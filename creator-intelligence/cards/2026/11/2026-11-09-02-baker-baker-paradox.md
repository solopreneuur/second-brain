# The job sticks, the surname doesn't

**The find:** Psychologist Gillian Cohen showed people the same photo, telling half the group the man's surname was "Baker" and the other half his job was "baker." Days later, far more people remembered "baker" the occupation than "Baker" the name.
**The surprise:** It's the identical word, used two different ways, producing wildly different memory — proof that the everyday habit of forgetting a name seconds after an introduction isn't a memory failure in general, just a failure at one specific kind of word.
**The idea:** The [Baker/baker paradox](../../../concepts/baker-baker-paradox.md): occupations plug into a rich web of associations (flour, ovens, bread) that help memory grab them back; a surname is an arbitrary tag with no such web.

## In depth

Psychologist Gillian Cohen, at the Open University's Human Cognition Research Laboratory, published "Why Is It Difficult to Put Names to Faces?" in the *British Journal of Psychology* (Vol. 81, No. 3, August 1990, pp. 287-297). She ran two experiments in which participants studied photographs of unfamiliar faces paired with three kinds of information: names, occupations, and possessions. Recall of names was consistently the worst — no better than recall of meaningless, made-up possessions, and well below recall of meaningful occupations or possessions. When she made names semantically meaningful and occupations meaningless, the deficit reversed, confirming that meaning, not word-type, drove the gap. Cohen built on a 1987 study by Kay McWeeny, Andrew Young, Dennis Hay, and Andrew Ellis ("Putting Names to Faces," *British Journal of Psychology*, Vol. 78, No. 2), who had shown subjects faces labeled with surnames or occupations and found surnames recalled far worse — even when the exact same word, like "Baker," served as both. Cohen's 1990 paper gave that effect its name: the Baker/baker paradox.

The explanation is about semantic richness, not sound. The occupation "baker" plugs into a dense network of associations already in memory — flour, ovens, dough, the smell of bread, a shopfront, an apron. Any one of those cues can trigger retrieval. The surname "Baker" is an arbitrary tag pointing at one specific person and nothing else; there's no web of related concepts to fish it back out with. This is why proper names are uniquely fragile in memory even though phonologically identical to common nouns.

The finding slotted into a larger literature on proper-name retrieval. Deborah Burke and Donald MacKay's 1991 *Journal of Memory and Language* study found that names were the single largest category of tip-of-the-tongue blocks, especially for acquaintances not seen recently. Researchers including Serge Brédart have since studied why face recognition, occupation recall, and name recall dissociate so cleanly, reinforcing that the bottleneck is specifically the arbitrary, non-semantic nature of proper names — not general forgetting, face-processing failure, or inattention.
