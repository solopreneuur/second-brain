# Real footage says bystanders usually help

**The find:** Researchers pulled 219 real CCTV-filmed public conflicts and fights from Amsterdam, Cape Town, and Lancaster and checked how often a stranger actually stepped in to help.
**The surprise:** Someone intervened in 90.9% of real conflicts — almost the exact opposite of what the famous "bystander effect" predicts about crowds leaving victims to fend for themselves.
**The idea:** A [naturalistic replication](../../../concepts/naturalistic-replication.md) — checking a textbook lab finding against real-world footage, not a new staged study — can overturn decades of received wisdom in one dataset.

## In depth

The "bystander effect" is one of the most cited findings in all of social psychology, built on the 1964 murder of Kitty Genovese in New York and formalized by John Darley and Bibb Latané's 1968 lab experiments showing that people were less likely to help a person in apparent distress the more other bystanders were present — a phenomenon they called diffusion of responsibility. It has been taught in introductory psychology courses for over fifty years, usually illustrated with staged scenarios: an actor fakes a seizure or asks for help while confederates in the room ignore it, and researchers time how long it takes someone to react.

Marie Rosenkrantz Lindegaard, Mark Levine, Richard Philpot, and Lasse Liebst took a different approach entirely. Rather than staging anything, they obtained real closed-circuit television footage of actual public conflicts and physical altercations — arguments, fights, aggressive confrontations — captured by security cameras in three cities with very different cultures and crime rates: Amsterdam, Cape Town, and Lancaster, England. They coded 219 such incidents for whether, and how many, bystanders intervened: stepping between combatants, pulling someone away, calming things verbally, or otherwise acting to de-escalate.

The result, published in American Psychologist in 2020, was startling relative to the lab-based literature: in 90.9% of these real conflicts, at least one bystander intervened, and this held true across all three cities despite their different violent-crime rates and cultural norms — more bystanders present didn't reduce the odds of help, and in some conditions, more bystanders actually meant a higher chance someone would step in. The researchers argue the classic bystander-effect studies, built on ambiguous, low-stakes staged scenarios in artificial lab settings, likely don't generalize well to the much higher-stakes, unambiguous reality of a real public fight — where social cues about whether something is actually an emergency are far less confusing, and intervening carries real social (and sometimes physical) weight.

The finding doesn't erase diffusion of responsibility as a real phenomenon in some contexts, but it's a sharp caution against treating any one lab paradigm, however famous, as a universal law of crowd behavior — and a reminder that footage of the real thing can out-rank a thousand confederates in a waiting room.
