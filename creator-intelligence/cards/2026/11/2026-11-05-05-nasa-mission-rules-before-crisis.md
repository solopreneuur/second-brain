# NASA wrote its rules before the crisis

**The find:** Apollo-era Mission Control kept a binder of pre-agreed "Mission Rules" — exact go/no-go decisions written in advance for every failure controllers could imagine, so nothing was improvised live during a crisis.
**The surprise:** NASA's answer to real-time uncertainty wasn't trusting the smartest person in the room under pressure — it was removing real-time judgment wherever possible, by resolving the decision before any life was on the line.
**The idea:** [Self-imposed constraint](../../../concepts/self-imposed-constraint.md) applied to decision-making itself — binding your future judgment while your thinking is clear beats trusting your judgment once the stakes are live.

7 · Far-field · Founders · [NASA Oral History Project](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/kranzef-3-19-98.pdf)

## In depth

Flight director Gene Kranz and his Mission Control teams didn't wait for a problem to happen and then figure out what to do — they ran a continuous process of asking "what could go wrong here?" for every phase of a mission, and then writing down the exact corrective action in advance, before launch. The result was a thick binder of "Mission Rules": for a given sensor failure, a given trajectory deviation, a given system fault, the rule stated precisely what the crew and ground would do, agreed on by every relevant engineer and manager while the question was still hypothetical and nobody's adrenaline was running.

The reasoning behind this, as Kranz and his controllers described it in NASA's own oral history archive, was that judgment under real pressure is measurably worse than judgment under calm conditions — not because controllers were unskilled, but because fear, time pressure, and the visibility of a live crisis distort exactly the kind of probabilistic reasoning a mission abort decision requires. So the solution wasn't "hire people who stay calm" — it was to make calm-state judgment do all the work, and have the in-crisis job reduced to looking up which pre-written rule applied and executing it. When Apollo 13's oxygen tank exploded, controllers weren't improvising a rescue from scratch; they were working from a dense foundation of rules about power budgets, trajectory options, and system shutdowns that had already been fought out and settled on the ground, months earlier, by people with nothing at stake yet.

The principle generalizes directly to founders and operators: a pre-mortem, a written incident playbook, or a hard rule like "we never ship on a Friday" is the same move — binding your future, crisis-state self to a decision made by your calmer, better-informed past self. It's a deliberate admission that in-the-moment judgment, however smart, is a worse tool than judgment exercised in advance.
