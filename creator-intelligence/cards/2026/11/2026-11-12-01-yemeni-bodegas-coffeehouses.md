# Yemeni bodegas built the coffeehouse boom

**The find:** Yemeni-Americans, a population smaller than 1/40th of 1% of the US, own roughly half of New York City's bodegas. In the last decade, the children of those bodega owners have opened a wave of Yemeni coffeehouses nationwide, using the family bodega's landlord relationships, supplier trust and interest-free community loans to skip the hardest parts of opening a new business.
**The surprise:** A tiny community didn't just succeed in one low-margin category, it built a private, inheritable "starter kit" of capital, real estate and trust that let the next generation launch an entirely different business almost for free.
**The idea:** One generation's infrastructure becomes the next generation's unfair advantage ([Piggyback infrastructure](../../../concepts/piggyback-infrastructure.md)).

## In depth

Hakim Sulaimani opened Yafa Cafe in Brooklyn in 2019, three doors down from Yemen Deli, the bodega his father Tarek had run since 1995. He didn't need to raise capital the way a typical founder would: his father's decades in the neighborhood had already built the relationships with the landlord, the foot traffic, and a reputation for reliability that any lender or supplier would otherwise want proof of. Hakim sourced coffee by calling cousins back in Yemen, and the café's lease terms came easier because of who his father was to that landlord.

This is not an isolated story. Yemeni-Americans are a minuscule share of the US population, yet dominate New York City's bodega trade, reportedly owning close to half of the city's roughly 5,000 corner stores. That dominance traces back to earlier waves of Yemeni immigration, chain migration through family and village networks, and informal lending circles that let new arrivals buy into existing stores without needing a bank. Over the past nine years, the children of that bodega generation have taken the same networks — capital, supplier contacts, real estate relationships — and redirected them into a new category: Yemeni coffeehouses serving a late-night, social, dessert-and-coffee experience aimed at a broader American audience, not just the immigrant community. Hakim's read on the opportunity was simple: "The demand for it. People saw a genuine demand for late-night eating and drinking. And Yemenis were in a good place to pick that up."

The mechanism generalizes well past bodegas: whenever one generation or cohort builds unglamorous infrastructure — distribution, trust, access, relationships — in one category, the next generation can often launch a completely unrelated business by plugging into that infrastructure instead of building their own from scratch, turning decades of a "boring" business into instant velocity for a trendier one.
