# Petzoldt ranked the summit third, not first

**The find:** Paul Petzoldt, after a disastrous 1938 American K2 expedition, wrote a code he called "expedition behavior." The organization he later founded to teach it, NOLS, still runs on nine principles today, and none of the nine mention summiting, speed, or achievement at all. They're entirely about organization, honesty, and restraint — including a rule to help others but not routinely do their work for them.
**The surprise:** The leadership code built specifically to get climbers up mountains has nothing to say about climbing mountains; it reads like a conduct manual for any team under pressure.
**The idea:** The best-documented rules for team performance under stress often say nothing about the goal itself, only about how people treat each other while chasing it ([Cross-domain transfer](../../../concepts/cross-domain-transfer.md)).

## In depth

Paul Petzoldt was on the 1938 American expedition to K2, the first American attempt on the mountain, which ended without a summit and with serious interpersonal breakdowns among the climbers. The experience convinced him that most expedition failures weren't caused by weather or terrain but by how people behaved toward each other under stress — hoarding effort, refusing to carry a fair share, letting resentment build in silence. He coined the term "expedition behavior" for the discipline of staying functional, generous and trustworthy as a group under pressure, and defined it in his later writing (The New Wilderness Handbook, 1984) as "an awareness of the relationships which exist in the out-of-doors plus the motivation and character to be as concerned for others as one is for oneself."

Petzoldt went on to found the National Outdoor Leadership School (NOLS) in 1965, which still teaches expedition behavior as a core curriculum. Today's version distills it into nine principles: serve the mission and goals of the group; be as concerned for others as for yourself; treat everyone with dignity and respect; support leadership and growth in everyone; do your share and stay organized; help others but don't routinely do their work for them; model integrity by being honest and accountable; say yes and deliver, or say no clearly; and resolve conflict productively. None of the nine reference summiting, pace, or technical skill.

The "help others but don't routinely do their work" line is the sharpest one: it explicitly warns against a kind of over-functioning that looks generous but actually erodes the group, by letting weaker members avoid building their own competence and by quietly burning out whoever keeps covering for them. Outdoor writer Howard Tomb later elaborated the idea into a blunter, more concrete list — get up early without complaint, learn to cook competently, carry more than your fair share of weight, never make teammates search for your lost gear — all variations on the same theme: team failure is usually made of small daily frictions, not single dramatic errors.
