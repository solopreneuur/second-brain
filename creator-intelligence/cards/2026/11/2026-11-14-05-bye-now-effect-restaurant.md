# Saying "bye" makes you buy more

**The find:** Davis and Herr found that priming diners with the word "goodbye" before ordering activated the sound-alike word "buy," and distracted diners who saw it rated the meal higher and said they'd pay more for it than diners primed with "so long."
**The surprise:** A goodbye phrase, not a discount or a menu trick, moved willingness to pay — purely because it sounds like a different word.
**The idea:** The bye-now effect — homophones can prime behavior through sound alone, especially when the listener is distracted or under cognitive load ([the bye-now effect](../../../concepts/bye-now-effect.md)).

## In depth

In 2014, the Journal of Consumer Research published "From Bye to Buy: Homophones as a Phonological Route to Priming" by Derick F. Davis (University of Miami) and Paul M. Herr (Virginia Tech). The core experiment ran through online panels — 175 participants recruited via Amazon Mechanical Turk, 47% women, ages 18 to 77 — who were first loaded with a cognitive task (memorizing a nine-digit number) and then shown a restaurant ad reading "Enjoy Tonight, Say 'Goodbye [So Long] to Everything Else." Those primed with "goodbye" rated the meal as more valuable (average rating 5.26 vs. 4.33–4.77 for controls) and reported being willing to pay $58.43 for it, versus $47.18 in the "so long" condition — roughly a 24% jump, a result Davis and Herr called statistically robust (p<.001) across two experiments with replications.

The mechanism they propose is "imperfect homophone suppression": reading or hearing "bye" automatically activates the sound-alike "buy" in memory, and under cognitive load — distraction, multitasking, a busy checkout line — the brain fails to fully suppress that unwanted activation, so the purchase-related meaning leaks into judgment and willingness to pay. Critically, this works through sound, not meaning: a purely semantic account of priming couldn't explain why "bye" alone — stripped of any purchase-related content — nudges people toward spending. The effect has since been folded into behavioral-marketing playbooks: writers at outlets like The Decision Lab and various marketing newsletters cite it as a reason brands build "bye bye" into names of beauty and skincare product lines, or into checkout-page copy ("say bye to breakouts"), on the theory that a shopper's own brain will quietly supply "buy" at the moment of highest cognitive load — the final click.
