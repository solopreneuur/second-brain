# FedEx's founder bet the company on blackjack

**The find:** In 1973, with Federal Express down to its last $5,000 and General Dynamics' board refusing more capital, 28-year-old founder Fred Smith flew to Las Vegas and bet the company's entire remaining cash at a blackjack table instead of going home.
**The surprise:** He won $27,000 — exactly enough to cover that week's jet fuel bill — and when his COO confronted him about risking the last of the money, Smith's answer was that without the fuel, the planes weren't flying anyway.
**The idea:** When the "safe" choice guarantees the same loss as the reckless one, the reckless choice stops being reckless — it's the only move left with any chance of a different outcome.

## In depth

Fred Smith founded Federal Express in June 1971, putting in roughly $4 million of his own inheritance and raising another sizeable sum from a syndicate that included General Dynamics, Prudential Insurance and several banks, arranged through the investment firm New Court Securities. Operations began April 17, 1973, with 14 small Falcon jets flying out of Memphis to 25 cities; the first night's haul was a modest handful of packages. The one-carrier, pickup-through-delivery model was unproven, and the 1973 Arab oil embargo hit almost immediately, driving jet fuel costs up sharply just as the young company was trying to find its footing.

By mid-1974 Federal Express was losing more than $1 million a month. Paychecks were sometimes postdated, and pilots occasionally put fuel on personal credit cards. The crisis peaked when Smith flew to Chicago to ask General Dynamics' board for more capital and was turned down flat. Fuel suppliers were demanding cash in advance every Monday just to keep the planes flying, and the company's account held about $5,000 — not enough for the next week's fuel. Instead of flying back to Memphis, Smith detoured to Las Vegas and sat down at a blackjack table with the company's last $5,000. He won $27,000. Company COO Roger Frock recounts in his memoir "Changing How the World Does Business" that when he confronted Smith about risking the last of the money, Smith shrugged that without funds for the fuel companies, they couldn't have flown anyway — so there was nothing left to lose by trying.

The win bought about a week, not a turnaround. In the weeks after, Smith secured roughly $11 million in emergency financing from existing investors, on harsh terms with heavy equity dilution, which let the company ride out 1974. Federal Express hit monthly profitability in February 1975 and posted its first full-year profit in 1976: $3.6 million on $75 million in revenue, helped by the "absolutely, positively overnight" campaign. By 1978 it was moving tens of thousands of packages nightly across dozens of airports. Smith died in June 2025 at 80; multiple independent sources corroborate the Vegas episode as true.
