# A crashed bomber built the surgery checklist

**The find:** In 1935 an expert pilot crashed a prototype B-17 bomber after forgetting to release a new control lock. The fix wasn't more training — it was a short index-card checklist, after which the fleet flew 1.8 million miles with zero accidents. Decades later, surgeon Atul Gawande and the Boeing engineer who helped design that checklist built the WHO Surgical Safety Checklist, cutting major surgical complications from 11% to 7% across eight hospitals.
**The surprise:** The fix for expert failure under complexity wasn't a smarter expert — it was a piece of paper that experts initially resisted as beneath them.
**The idea:** [Cross-domain transfer](../../../concepts/cross-domain-transfer.md) — checklists beat expertise under complexity and distraction, whether the stakes are a cockpit or an operating table.

## Deeper · 2026-11-03

On October 30, 1935, at Wright Field outside Dayton, Ohio, the Army Air Corps ran the final evaluation flight in a bomber competition under specification 98-201. Three manufacturers had entered: Martin's Model 146, Douglas's DB-1, and Boeing's Model 299, an experimental four-engine aircraft nicknamed the "Flying Fortress" by a Seattle reporter because it could carry five times the bomb load the Army had asked for. At the controls sat Major Ployer P. Hill, the Army's chief test pilot, flying the 299 for the first time; beside him was Lieutenant Donald Putt, who had flown it on every previous test flight. Boeing's own chief test pilot, Leslie Tower, plus a Boeing mechanic and a Pratt & Whitney engine representative, rode along. The plane taxied, took off normally, climbed to around 300 feet — then stalled, rolled onto one wing, and crashed into a fireball.

Putt, the mechanic, and the engine rep escaped with burns. Hill and Tower were trapped; First Lieutenant Robert Giovannoli ran into the burning wreck twice to pull them out (he was later awarded the Cheney Medal for it, posthumously — he died in a separate crash before it was presented). Both Hill and Tower died of their injuries. The investigation found no mechanical fault: Hill had forgotten to release the gust lock, a new mechanism on this aircraft that clamped the elevator and rudder controls so wind couldn't damage them on the ground. Airborne, with the controls locked, the plane was unflyable. Tower reportedly realized it and tried to reach the release handle too late. Newspapers ran with the verdict that the 299 was "too much airplane for one man to fly."

The verdict had teeth: the Army's bulk order, 133 aircraft, went to the simpler Douglas DB-1 (which became the B-18 Bolo). Boeing nearly went under. Only after Air Corps officers lobbied on the plane's behalf did it get a lifeline — a "service test" order for 13 more Model 299s, delivered to the 2nd Bombardment Group at Langley Field by August 1937, watched closely for any further incident that would kill the program outright. Facing that pressure, the unit's pilots didn't ask for more training on an aircraft that had just killed two of the most experienced test pilots in the Air Corps. They wrote down every step: four checklists — takeoff, flight, before landing, after landing — short enough to fit on an index card. With them, the 2nd Bombardment Group's dozen aircraft flew 1.8 million miles without a serious accident, according to the Air Force's own archived history of the episode. The Army went on to order 12,731 of what became the B-17.

The medical connection runs through Atul Gawande, the Harvard surgeon and New Yorker writer who led the World Health Organization's "Safe Surgery Saves Lives" program starting around 2007. Designing a checklist for operating rooms, Gawande's team deliberately borrowed from aviation's seven decades of checklist design, and brought in Daniel J. Boorman — a Boeing flight-deck engineer, not a pilot — as a named co-author on the methodology paper, "Perspectives in Quality: Designing the WHO Surgical Safety Checklist" (International Journal for Quality in Health Care, 2010). The result was a 19-item, three-phase checklist (Sign In, before anesthesia; Time Out, before incision; Sign Out, before the patient leaves the room), officially launched in Washington, D.C. on June 25, 2008.

The trial ran October 2007 to September 2008 across eight hospitals in eight cities spanning rich and poor settings alike — Toronto, New Delhi, Amman, Auckland, Manila, Ifakara (Tanzania), London, and Seattle. Researchers tracked 3,733 surgical patients before the checklist and 3,955 after. Complication rates fell from 11.0% to 7.0% (p<0.001); in-hospital death rates fell from 1.5% to 0.8% (p=0.003) — both gains held in high-income and low-income sites alike. The results ran in the New England Journal of Medicine in January 2009. Gawande said afterward that "not everyone on the operating teams were happy to try it. But the results were unprecedented. And the teams became strong supporters" (WHO press release, December 2010). A later independent study of 357 hospitals in 58 countries found checklist use tied to 38% lower odds of death within 30 days of emergency abdominal surgery — though Wikipedia's own summary of the research notes some hospitals saw no measurable improvement at all, usually tied to poor staff buy-in rather than the checklist itself failing.
