# Alibaba's free Taobao beat eBay's paid model

**The find:** When Taobao launched in 2003 to compete with eBay in China, Alibaba made it free — no listing fees, no transaction fees — against eBay's proven paid-auction model, a call that looked financially unworkable. eBay's CEO Meg Whitman dismissed the free upstart as "a bunch of small competitors nipping at our heels."
**The surprise:** Free was the model that worked: Taobao's share of Chinese online auctions passed two-thirds within about three years, and by the end of 2006 eBay had exited the Chinese market entirely.
**The idea:** [Incumbent complacency](../../../concepts/incumbent-complacency.md) — a confident dismissal of a "money-losing" business model is sometimes the market leader's last public statement before losing the market.

## In depth

In 2002, eBay bought a one-third stake in Shanghai-based EachNet, China's leading consumer auction site, for $30 million; in 2003 it acquired the rest, paying roughly $150 million total. At the time EachNet held upwards of 85% of Chinese online auction volume. That same year, Jack Ma's Alibaba launched Taobao, explicitly to block eBay from controlling Chinese e-commerce. Where eBay charged listing and transaction fees — its core global business model — Taobao let sellers list for free, a policy Ma kept in place for years even as eBay's own team argued "free" wasn't a real business model. Taobao also built in instant messaging between buyers and sellers, matching how Chinese shoppers wanted to haggle and build trust, something eBay's auction format didn't support.

The market-share reversal was fast and well documented: Taobao held about 7.8% of China's C2C market in 2003 against eBay-EachNet's 72.4%; by 2005 Taobao had risen to 58.6%; by March 2006 Taobao had reached roughly two-thirds, while eBay-EachNet had fallen to about 29%. At eBay's February 2005 analyst day, and in an April 2005 Forbes profile, CEO Meg Whitman dismissed the threat, calling local rivals "a bunch of small competitors nipping at our heels," even as eBay pledged $100 million that year to defend the market. In December 2006, eBay folded EachNet into a joint venture with TOM Online — eBay held 49% and contributed $40 million, TOM Online held 51%, contributed $20 million, and took management control. By mid-2007 the venture's share had fallen to about 7%. eBay had sunk close to $300 million into Chinese operations.

Jack Ma described the strategy as: "eBay may be a shark in the ocean, but I'm a crocodile in the Yangtze River. If we fight in the ocean, we lose, but if we fight in the river, we win." Postmortems point to eBay routing EachNet's servers through the US, centralizing product decisions in San Jose, and refusing to localize communication and payment features the way Taobao did.
