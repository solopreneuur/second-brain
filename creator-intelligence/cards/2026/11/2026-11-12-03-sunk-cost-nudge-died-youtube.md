# A sunk-cost nudge that died on YouTube

**The find:** Researchers ran a live YouTube field experiment on 11,328 real viewers, randomizing the wait before a skip button appears (0, 5 or 10 seconds) to test whether a longer wait (more "invested" time) makes people more likely to keep watching the ad. In their own lab version the sunk-cost effect was real but small. On actual YouTube, with real viewers in real moments, it had no detectable effect at all.
**The surprise:** One of the most reliable biases in behavioural economics, replicated for decades in small lab studies, evaporates when it has to survive contact with a real platform and real distracted viewers.
**The idea:** A lab effect and a field effect are not the same claim, and most "proven" biases have never been tested at the scale or mess of the real world ([Sunk-cost fallacy](../../../concepts/sunk-cost-fallacy.md)).

## In depth

The sunk-cost fallacy — the tendency to keep going with something because of what you've already invested, rather than what it will return — is one of the most frequently cited findings in behavioural economics. Economists Emil Beknazar-Yuzbashev, Dorothea Ichiba and Marta Stalinski set out to test it with unusual rigor: not just a classroom vignette, but a matched pair of experiments, one inside a lab and one running live on YouTube itself.

In the lab arm (N=1,806), the design mimicked a classic sunk-cost setup: participants were randomly assigned different "invested" wait times before being allowed to move on to a preferred task, and the researchers measured whether a longer imposed wait made people stick around longer afterward. They did find a real effect here — about 0.12 standard deviations, equivalent to roughly 1.3 extra minutes of continued engagement for a cost difference worth about $2 on a 14-minute task. Small, but statistically real, consistent with decades of lab literature.

The field arm is the striking part. The team worked with YouTube to randomize the actual wait time before the "Skip Ad" button becomes clickable (0 vs 5 vs 10 seconds) for over 11,000 real viewers encountering real ads in their normal viewing sessions. If sunk cost worked the way lab studies suggest, a longer forced wait should make people more likely to keep watching once they could skip. It didn't. The intervention produced no statistically significant effect on engagement at all.

The gap between the two results is the finding. It's a caution against assuming a well-replicated psychological effect will survive once it has to compete with everything else going on in someone's actual day — distraction, habit, low stakes, a different context entirely. For marketers and product designers who build sunk-cost mechanics (progress bars, invested-time messaging, "don't lose your streak" prompts) assuming lab-sized effects, the real lift at scale may be far smaller or absent.
