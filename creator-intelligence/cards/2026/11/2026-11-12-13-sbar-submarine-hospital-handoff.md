# A submarine officer redesigned the hospital handoff

**The find:** SBAR, the Situation-Background-Assessment-Recommendation script now standard for hospital nursing handoffs worldwide, was built in 2002 by Doug Bonacum, a former US Navy nuclear-submarine safety officer, together with Dr. Michael Leonard at Kaiser Permanente. Bonacum ported the terse briefing structure submarine crews use to prevent reactor and collision errors directly into how nurses hand off patients between shifts.
**The surprise:** The fix for a well-documented, deadly source of hospital error, miscommunication during handoffs, didn't come from inside medicine. It came from how a submarine crew structures a sentence during a crisis so nothing gets lost between people.
**The idea:** A format built to survive a different kind of catastrophe can be ported wholesale into a new field, because the actual problem (critical information lost in translation between people) is identical ([Cross-domain transfer](../../../concepts/cross-domain-transfer.md)).

## In depth

Miscommunication during shift handoffs has long been identified as one of the largest preventable causes of medical error: a nurse or doctor ending a shift knows things about a patient that the next one doesn't, and under time pressure, the handoff conversation is exactly the kind of unstructured, rushed exchange where critical details get dropped. Hospitals had tried various fixes for this before, mostly longer or more detailed handoff notes, which tended to bury the important information rather than surface it.

The fix that stuck came from outside medicine entirely. Doug Bonacum had spent part of his career as a safety officer on US Navy nuclear submarines, where crews rely on an extremely terse, standardized reporting format to communicate urgent information during high-stakes moments, because a submarine crew doesn't have the luxury of a rambling update when something is wrong with the reactor or a collision is imminent. Working with physician Michael Leonard at Kaiser Permanente in 2002, Bonacum adapted that military brevity code into a four-part structure for clinical handoffs: Situation (what's happening with the patient right now), Background (relevant clinical context), Assessment (what you think is going on), and Recommendation (what you think should happen next).

SBAR spread quickly through US and UK hospital systems and is now taught as a near-universal standard in nursing education, specifically because it forces exactly the information a replacement needs, in a predictable order, regardless of who's giving or receiving the handoff. Its origin is a useful case for a general principle: the right fix for "critical information gets lost when handed between people under time pressure" doesn't need to be invented inside the field that has the problem. Submarines, aviation, and the military have been solving variations of this exact problem for far longer than most civilian fields have had to.
