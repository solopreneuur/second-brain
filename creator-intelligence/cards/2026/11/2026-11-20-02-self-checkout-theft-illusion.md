# Self-checkout was never about speed

**The find:** Self-checkout isn't actually faster than a trained cashier, since shoppers lack a cashier's memorized product codes — but the busywork of scanning makes the wait *feel* shorter. Meanwhile about 1 in 7 shoppers admit to intentionally stealing through it, costing US retailers an estimated $10 billion a year, and store staffing has crept back up rather than down.
**The surprise:** The technology sold on labor savings ended up requiring more staff — to police theft and unjam machines — quietly undercutting the business case that got it installed in the first place.
**The idea:** Busywork that makes you feel productive is a [labor illusion](../../../concepts/labor-illusion.md) — the feeling of effort, not the actual efficiency, is what's really being sold.

## In depth

Self-checkout machines arrived in US grocery stores in the late 1990s, pitched explicitly as a labor-cost play: fewer cashiers, faster lines, lower overhead. Three decades later, the economics look far murkier than the pitch suggested. Drew University sociologist Christopher Andrews, who has studied self-checkout labor for years, points out that a trained cashier who has memorized hundreds of product look-up codes and barcodes is measurably faster at the physical task of scanning than an untrained shopper fumbling with a touchscreen, a bagging-area scale, and an unfamiliar interface. The actual throughput gain many stores expected from self-checkout never fully materialized once you account for shoppers who struggle, machines that misread items, and the attendant hovering nearby to help.

What self-checkout does reliably change is the shopper's *perception* of the wait. Having something to do with your hands — scanning, bagging, confirming — makes idle time feel like it's passing faster, even when the clock says otherwise, a phenomenon retail researchers describe using language adjacent to the "labor illusion": people trust and value a process more when they can see (or do) the work involved, even if the work adds no real speed.

The theft side of the ledger is where the self-checkout bet looks worst. Industry surveys cited on the episode suggest roughly one in seven shoppers has intentionally stolen something while using self-checkout — scanning a cheaper item's barcode for an expensive one, skipping a scan entirely, or simply walking a non-scanned item past the sensor gates — contributing to an estimated $10 billion or more in annual US retail shrinkage tied to the format. Target, Walmart, Dollar General, and others have all either scaled back self-checkout lanes, added more attendants, or reintroduced staffed-only lanes in response, which is the opposite of the labor reduction the technology was originally sold to deliver. The lesson retailers are relearning in real time: removing a human from a transaction doesn't remove the need for human oversight — it just relocates where that oversight has to happen.
