# People shocked themselves rather than think

**The find:** Participants sat alone in a bare room for 6-15 minutes with nothing to do but think, after saying in advance they'd pay money to avoid a mild electric shock. Given a button that delivered that same shock, 67% of men and 25% of women pressed it at least once rather than just sit with their thoughts.
**The surprise:** People chose a pain they'd already said they wanted to avoid over unstructured time alone with their own mind.
**The idea:** [Boredom aversion](../../../concepts/boredom-aversion.md) — being alone with nothing to do is unpleasant enough that many people will trade it for something actively worse.

2 · Shocking number · Psychology · [Wilson et al., "Just think: The challenges of the disengaged mind," Science, 2014](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1250830)

## In depth

Timothy Wilson and seven coauthors at the University of Virginia and Harvard ran 11 studies testing whether people enjoy being alone with their thoughts. The core procedure: sit in an empty room with no phone and no reading material for 6-15 minutes, just "entertain yourself with your thoughts." Across the studies, about half of participants rated the experience as not very enjoyable, 58% found it hard to concentrate, and 89% said their mind wandered. A version run at home found people enjoyed it even less there, and roughly a third admitted breaking the rules — checking a phone, getting up — despite being told not to.

The electric-shock study that got the headlines involved a specific 42-person subset: people who had already tried a mild shock and said beforehand they'd pay money to avoid repeating it. During a 15-minute solo thinking period with a shock button available, 67% of men (12 of 18) shocked themselves at least once, versus 25% of women (6 of 24). The average number of shocks was 1.47 for men (one outlier pressed it 190 times) and 1.00 for women. The paper's own explanation for the sex gap is thin — it points to men scoring higher on average on "sensation-seeking," without elaborating.

This isn't a failed-replication story. A 2018 Nature Human Behaviour mass-replication project and a 2023 student replication project both reproduced the directional effect, and Wilson's own 2018 cross-cultural follow-up — 2,557 people across 12 sites in 11 countries — found people everywhere enjoyed solitary "doing" more than solitary "thinking." The real pushback is interpretive: a 2014 commentary in Frontiers in Psychology pointed out that the original data's mean enjoyment rating was actually mildly positive, not aversive, that 57% of participants never shocked themselves at all, and that most who did cited curiosity rather than escape as their reason. Wilson published a direct rebuttal defending the original framing.
