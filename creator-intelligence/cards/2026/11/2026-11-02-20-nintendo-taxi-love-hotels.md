# Nintendo ran taxis and love hotels

**The find:** Before video games, Hiroshi Yamauchi pushed Nintendo into a taxi company, individually-portioned instant rice, and a chain of love hotels in the 1960s and 70s, all of which failed, before the company pivoted to toys and then games.
**The surprise:** The company now synonymous with Mario spent over a decade as a failed taxi-and-love-hotel conglomerate before anyone there had made a single video game.
**The idea:** [Narrative fallacy](../../../concepts/narrative-fallacy.md): the "pivot" story only reads as destiny in hindsight; at the time it was just trial and error.

## Deeper · 2026-11-02

Hiroshi Yamauchi took over Nintendo in 1949 at age 22, dropping out of Waseda University after his grandfather, Sekiryo Kaneda, suffered a stroke and retired. He was the third president of a company that had been making hanafuda playing cards since its founding by Fusajiro Yamauchi in 1889, a product tangled up with Japan's gambling underworld — Nintendo's original Kyoto headquarters sat in territory controlled by the Aizukotetsukai yakuza syndicate, and engineer Gunpei Yokoi later recalled "people from the local mafia would often come to Nintendo, very angry" when defective cards turned up at gambling tables. Yamauchi modernized the card business, striking a deal to print Disney characters on playing cards in 1959 and taking Nintendo public in 1962, but he saw the ceiling on a niche product and started pushing the company sideways. Between roughly 1963 and 1968 he ran a burst of unrelated ventures: a taxi company called Daiya ("Diamond"), a line of individually portioned instant rice sold under the San-O Foods brand, and, according to many retellings, a love hotel chain, alongside smaller flops in instant noodle soup, toy blocks, ball-point pens, baby swings, TV dinners, and even photocopiers.

The taxi venture is the best documented of the group. Daiya ran a fleet that grew to roughly 40 cars, painted in Yamauchi's preferred blue with Nintendo's kanji on the door — a 1971 photo shows future Metroid producer Satoru Okada standing next to one. It wasn't a market failure so much as a labor and regulatory one: drivers unionized and pushed for raises that made the business too expensive to run profitably, and the venture is frequently tied to the disruption around the 1964 Tokyo Olympics, when the city tightened taxi licensing and competition intensified. Yamauchi eventually sold it off. The instant rice business failed on product quality — contemporary accounts describe the dehydrated, individually portioned rice as simply not tasty, and Nintendo was competing against companies that already had food-industry expertise and distribution. Nintendo reportedly lost significant money on it before quietly dropping the line. The love hotel chain is the shakiest claim in the whole story: it's repeated constantly online, sometimes alongside the suggestion that Yamauchi, who had multiple mistresses, had a personal interest in the business, but serious retrospectives — including Kotaku's deep dive into Nintendo's buried history — note there's no verified documentation of Nintendo actually owning or operating a hotel, and some researchers now treat it as urban legend layered onto real facts about the taxi and rice ventures.

The pivot came almost by accident. Yokoi, an electronics maintenance worker on the playing-card assembly line, built an extendable mechanical-arm toy as a personal project; Yamauchi spotted it in the factory in 1966, expected to fire Yokoi for goofing off, and instead ordered it into mass production. Released as the Ultra Hand, it sold over 1.2 million units, and Yamauchi promoted Yokoi to head Nintendo's first dedicated R&D division. That toy division spent the next decade churning out products — the Love Tester, Ultra Machine, and eventually Yokoi's Game & Watch handhelds — while Nintendo separately licensed Magnavox's Odyssey pong-style technology to build the Color TV-Game home console line starting in 1977, with engineer Masayuki Uemura, who'd joined Nintendo in 1972 after working with Yokoi on solar-cell toy components at Sharp, doing the technical work. Those consoles sold well enough that in 1981 Yamauchi phoned Uemura directly and green-lit a cartridge-based successor; it shipped in Japan in 1983 as the Famicom and in the US in 1985 as the Nintendo Entertainment System, with Donkey Kong (1981) having already proven Nintendo's arcade chops under Shigeru Miyamoto in between.

The "pivot" framing is a textbook case of what Nassim Taleb, in The Black Swan (2007), named the narrative fallacy: the human compulsion to retrofit a clean causal story onto a sequence of facts, making chaotic, undirected trial and error look like a predetermined arc toward an inevitable destination. Nintendo's own history supports this better than most examples, because the company spent close to a decade — taxis, rice, allegedly hotels, blocks, pens — failing at things with no discernible throughline before one unplanned factory-floor toy happened to stick. Other famous "origin pivot" stories get similarly flattened in hindsight: Nokia began as an 1865 Finnish wood-pulp mill, spent the mid-20th century as a sprawling conglomerate of rubber boots, cables, and power generation, and only became a phone company near the end of that process, not by any master plan. Wrigley started out selling soap and baking powder and threw in free chewing gum as a promotional gimmick that customers wanted more than the soap. Tiffany & Co. opened in 1837 as a stationery and fancy-goods shop before shifting to jewelry in 1853. Raytheon began in 1922 as the American Appliance Company, making refrigeration units, before its radio-tube business took over. DuPont started in 1802 as a gunpowder maker and only became a diversified chemicals giant after a century making explosives. In each case, popular retellings compress decades of improvisation into a tidy "they always knew" story — which is exactly the fallacy Nintendo's taxi-and-rice decade illustrates: the pivot only looks like destiny once you already know Mario happened.
