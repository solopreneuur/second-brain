# Cough syrup brags it tastes awful

**The find:** W.K. Buckley's cough mixture had stagnant sales when heir Frank Buckley became the on-camera face of a 1980s campaign built entirely around confessing, "It tastes awful. And it works."
**The surprise:** The entire selling proposition is the company admitting the thing a skeptical customer already suspects, instead of hiding it.
**The idea:** [Radical honesty](../../../concepts/radical-honesty.md) — naming the exact flaw a customer already fears removes their need to go looking for the catch, and the brand gets credited for honesty instead of penalized for the flaw.

3 · Wrong decision that worked · Marketing · [Buckley's company history](https://www.theptdc.com/articles/tastes-awful-works)

## In depth

William Knapp Buckley developed his cough mixture in Toronto in 1919, a thick, strong-tasting formula built around ammonium carbonate and Canada balsam that genuinely tastes unpleasant to most people. For decades the company sold it quietly as a trusted home remedy, with sales plateaued around $2 million a year. In the mid-1980s, Buckley's later-generation leadership approved a radical pitch from their agency: rather than mask or apologize for the taste, make it the entire campaign. Frank Buckley, a company executive, became the deadpan on-camera spokesman, closing every ad with some version of "It tastes awful. And it works." The honesty did two things at once — it pre-empted the obvious objection before a first-time buyer could raise it, and it implied the medicine must be strong precisely because it tastes so bad, leaning on the folk belief that unpleasant medicine is more potent. Sales grew steadily through the 1990s and 2000s, and the company expanded into the US and other English-speaking markets on the strength of the same line, which it kept largely unchanged for over two decades — an unusually long run for a single tagline. Buckley's was acquired by Novartis in 2001 and passed to GSK as part of a later consumer-health reshuffle, with the tagline surviving each change of ownership. The campaign is now a standard teaching example in advertising and copywriting courses for the idea that naming a real flaw, rather than hiding it, can become a brand's strongest asset.
