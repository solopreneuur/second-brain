# Daniel Ek's 500-Hour Rule

**The find:** Spotify co-founder Daniel Ek tells Steven Bartlett he spent roughly 500 hours just learning the music-licensing problem before he'd even committed to building Spotify, and later spent close to a decade quietly studying healthcare before founding his second company, Neko Health.
**The surprise:** Ek frames the founder's edge not as vision or risk appetite but as pure, unglamorous hour-count on one narrow problem — "the scarcest resource we have in the world today by far is time."
**The idea:** [Cross-domain transfer](../../../concepts/cross-domain-transfer.md) in reverse: deep, narrow research before acting is what manufactures the insight that looks like genius from the outside.

## In depth

Daniel Ek founded Spotify in 2006 with Martin Lorentzon, after selling his earlier online-marketing company Advertigo for $1.25 million at 23. By his own account he thought making a legal music-streaming service was a "terrible idea" for a two-year stretch, convinced piracy had already won — the stress reportedly cost him his hair and added 30 pounds before Spotify finally launched in 2008. What rarely makes the highlight reel is the method behind the eventual confidence: in the clip from his November 2026 Diary of a CEO appearance, Ek says that before writing a line of code or raising money, he put in roughly 500 hours studying music copyright specifically — mechanical rights, performance rights, and the granular ISRC codes used to track recordings — until he says he became "one of the most foremost experts" on the subject, enough to negotiate licensing deals the labels hadn't seen workable before.

He describes applying the same discipline to Neko Health, the Stockholm-based preventive-health company he co-founded in 2018 with Hjalmar Nilsonne. Ek says he spent nearly a decade turning the healthcare-diagnostics problem over in his head before he ever started the company, treating the long runway as the actual unlock rather than a delay. His framing inverts the usual founder mythology of fast pivots and bold bets: instead, he argues the edge comes from being willing to sit with a single, narrow problem — "a tiny prism" — for far longer than anyone else would tolerate, because that is what lets you see a solution competitors with more resources and less patience will miss entirely.
