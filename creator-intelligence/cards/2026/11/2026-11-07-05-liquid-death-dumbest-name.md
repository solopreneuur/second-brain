# Investors called the name "too dumb to fund"

**The find:** When Mike Cessario pitched canned water branded "Liquid Death" with a melting skull logo, beverage-industry contacts and investors said the cans "looked too much like beer" and that no retailer would stock something that says "Death" on it. He self-funded a $1,500 mock commercial instead; it hit about 3 million views and nearly 80,000 Facebook followers before a single can existed. By 2022 the company was doing around $130 million in sales at a $700 million valuation.
**The surprise:** The exact name and look that investors said would keep the product off shelves is now sold in tens of thousands of stores, including Kroger and Target.
**The idea:** [Anti-branding](../../../concepts/anti-branding.md): when a category is commoditized and attention is the real bottleneck, a name that "shouldn't work" can function as free distribution.

## In depth

Mike Cessario spent roughly a decade as an advertising creative director before founding Liquid Death — working at agencies including Crispin Porter + Bogusky and VaynerMedia, then as a creative director at Netflix, where he led marketing campaigns for "Stranger Things," "Narcos," and "House of Cards," plus viral work for Toyota and Mountain Dew. The idea traces to 2009, when he watched musicians at the Vans Warped Tour in Denver drinking water out of Monster Energy cans to stay hydrated without looking uncool onstage — he wondered why only beer and energy drinks got to look dangerous and fun while water was stuck looking clinical. He filed the "Liquid Death" trademark in July 2017 with a melting-skull logo on a tallboy can styled like a craft beer. When he shopped the concept to beverage-industry contacts and investors, the universal response was rejection: the cans "looked too much like beer" and would confuse shoppers, and retailers would never put something on a shelf that said "Death" on it. No investor would write a check for the concept alone.

So in 2018, with no product and no funding, Cessario took a 3D rendering of the can and shot a two-minute mock commercial for about $1,500, then spent a few thousand more in paid media behind it. Over roughly four to five months it reached about 3 million views and nearly 80,000 Facebook followers — more than Aquafina's entire Facebook following at the time — with hundreds of comments asking where to buy it, even though the can didn't exist yet. That traction became the pitch deck. In January 2019, Science Inc. led a $1.6 million seed round (investors included Twitter's Biz Stone and Away's Jen Rubio), and Liquid Death began selling direct-to-consumer that month. Growth then compounded through funding rounds: $9 million (2020), $23 million (2020), $75 million at a $525 million valuation (January 2022), $70 million at $700 million (October 2022), and $67 million at $1.4 billion (March 2024). Revenue went from about $10 million (2020) to roughly $130 million (2022) to about $333 million (2024), with distribution expanding from Whole Foods to 7-Eleven, Publix, Kroger, and Target. The clean growth story hides a real near-collapse: Liquid Death initially canned product in Austria and shipped it to the US, and when COVID-era ocean freight costs spiked, the company was losing money on every case sold — the faster it grew, the more it lost — forcing it to fix sourcing and logistics. The company has never reported a profitable year, underscoring that canned water is a heavy, low-margin product where freight and aluminum costs eat most of the markup regardless of how hot the brand is.
