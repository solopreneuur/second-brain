# The scurvy cure that got lost

**The find:** The Royal Navy cured scurvy with citrus in the 1700s, then switched from lemons to West Indian limes and boiled the juice through copper pipes — both of which destroyed the vitamin C nobody yet knew was the active ingredient. The "cure" quietly stopped working, and scurvy returned on later expeditions, including Scott's fatal 1911-12 Antarctic trek.
**The surprise:** A solved medical problem came back and killed people a century later, because the solution was followed as ritual without anyone understanding why it worked.
**The idea:** [Ritual without understanding](../../../concepts/ritual-without-understanding.md): a fix survives only as long as someone understands the mechanism behind it. Lose the why, and the ritual silently breaks.

## In depth

In 1747, Royal Navy surgeon James Lind ran one of the first controlled clinical trials aboard HMS Salisbury, giving twelve scorbutic sailors six different treatments. The two given oranges and lemons recovered fastest. Lind published the results in 1753, but he and the naval medical establishment treated citrus as just one remedy among several, ranking it below "fresh air" and exercise, and the Navy did not act on it for decades. In 1795, physician Gilbert Blane, head of the Sick and Wounded Board, persuaded the Admiralty to mandate three-quarters of an ounce of lemon juice daily for every sailor, mixed into grog with alcohol as a preservative. Scurvy largely vanished from the fleet.

Around 1860, the Navy switched from Mediterranean lemons to West Indian limes. The reasoning was economic and imperial: lemons came from foreign suppliers and had to be bought with hard currency, while limes could be grown in British Caribbean colonies. Nobody at the time understood the active ingredient, so "lemon juice" and "lime juice" were treated as interchangeable — the word stuck as the slang "limey" for British sailors. But limes carry only about a quarter to half the vitamin C of lemons. Worse, the lime juice was stored in settling tanks exposed to air and pumped through copper tubing; copper ions catalyze the oxidation of ascorbic acid, and heat, light, and air exposure degrade it further. By the 1870s, sailors were drinking juice with little to no antiscorbutic value, and scurvy outbreaks returned on some ships, discrediting citrus as a reliable cure in many naval and medical minds.

This confusion persisted into the Edwardian polar expeditions, which carried lime juice as their antiscorbutic, believing it protective. Robert Falcon Scott's 1911-12 Terra Nova expedition suffered weakness, failure of wounds to heal, and Edgar Evans's collapse and death on the Beardmore Glacier descent — symptoms consistent with scurvy, though historians disagree on how definitive the diagnosis is. Vilhjalmur Stefansson's Canadian Arctic Expedition (1913-18) also lost men to scurvy. The mechanistic resolution came through Axel Holst and Theodor Frølich's 1907 guinea pig experiments establishing scurvy as a dietary deficiency, Casimir Funk's 1912 "vitamine" concept, and finally the isolation of the compound itself: Charles Glen King's lab and Albert Szent-Györgyi both identified ascorbic acid as vitamin C within weeks of each other in spring 1932 — finally explaining what citrus had been delivering, and what boiling, copper, and the wrong fruit had been quietly destroying.
