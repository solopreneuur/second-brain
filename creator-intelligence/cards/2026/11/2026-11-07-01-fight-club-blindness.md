# Officer ran past a beating and never saw it

**The find:** Boston officer Kenny Conley chased a suspect past a brutal assault on a fellow officer and swore under oath he never saw it — and was convicted of perjury for the claim. Researchers restaged the chase: volunteers ran after someone for three minutes past a staged fight 8 meters off the path. At night, only 35% noticed it; even in daylight, just 56% did.
**The surprise:** The study wasn't testing honesty, it was testing the physics of attention — and it found that missing a nearby fight while running a focused chase is the normal outcome, not a lie.
**The idea:** [Inattentional blindness](../../../concepts/inattentional-blindness.md): focused pursuit narrows perception so completely that a violent, multi-person event in plain sight can go entirely unseen.

2 · Shocking number · Psychology · [i-Perception, 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3485775/)

## In depth

In January 1995, Boston police officer Kenny Conley chased a murder suspect through the streets of Roxbury after a shooting outside a nightclub on Blue Hill Avenue. Somewhere along that chase, a group of fellow officers — mistaking an undercover colleague, Michael Cox, for one of the fleeing suspects — beat Cox unconscious. Conley ran past the beating. When grand juries investigated the cover-up, Conley testified under oath that he hadn't seen it. Prosecutors didn't believe him: he was indicted in 1997, convicted in 2000 of perjury and obstruction of justice, and sentenced to 34 months in prison. The case became a symbol of police "blue wall of silence" culture — a cop supposedly lying to protect his own. His conviction was eventually thrown out (a federal judge ruled he hadn't gotten a fair trial, and in 2011 the government dropped the case rather than retry him), and a separate civil jury also cleared him. He later rejoined the Boston Police Department and, in recent years, holds rank as a sergeant detective.

In 2011, Harvard psychologist Christopher Chabris, with Adam Weinberger, Matthew Fontaine, and Daniel Simons, published "You Do Not Talk About Fight Club if You Do Not Notice Fight Club" in the journal i-Perception to test whether Conley's claim was psychologically plausible rather than a lie. They had participants run after a confederate for about three minutes along a real campus path, replicating the chase. Along the route, a staged fight — three confederates — broke out roughly 8 meters off the path. Running the test at night, only 35% of participants noticed the fight; in daylight, 56% noticed. Adding a dual cognitive task (tracking two separate counts, mimicking an officer's divided attention while pursuing a suspect) further reduced noticing. In the condition closest to Conley's actual circumstances — nighttime, under cognitive load, while chasing someone — a majority of ordinary people missed an ongoing assault happening in plain sight just off their path.

This sits in the same research lineage as Simons and Chabris's famous 1999 "invisible gorilla" experiment, where observers asked to count basketball passes in a video missed a person in a gorilla suit walking through the scene. Both studies demonstrate inattentional blindness: people routinely fail to perceive fully visible objects or events when their attention is focused elsewhere, even at full alertness. The Conley study extended that lab finding into a real-world, high-stakes forensic application — directly challenging the legal and juror assumption that "if it happened in front of you, you must have seen it," with implications for how courts weigh eyewitness and bystander testimony.
