# The rule that overrides a captain

**The find:** After simulator research found about 25% of co-pilots would let a captain fly a plane into the ground rather than challenge him, airlines wrote the "Two-Challenge Rule": a first officer must challenge the captain twice, and if there's still no response, is required to take control of the aircraft.
**The surprise:** The fix doesn't ask junior staff to be braver — it removes the judgment call entirely, making a second unanswered challenge an automatic, scripted trigger to seize control.
**The idea:** Build hierarchy-overriding escalation into the system in advance, because in the moment nobody junior will decide on their own that the boss is wrong ([the two-challenge rule](../../../concepts/two-challenge-rule.md)).

## In depth

In the early 1970s, researchers ran simulator trials in which a captain was secretly instructed to feign "subtle incapacitation" — a stroke-like state where he looked and sounded fine but was flying the plane into danger — during a critical phase like approach or landing. The result: in roughly 25% of trials, the first officer let the aircraft hit the simulated ground rather than challenge the captain outright. In the other 75%, crews still took between 30 seconds and 4 minutes to recognize the problem and act. The finding exposed a basic flaw in cockpit hierarchy: co-pilots were trained to defer, and deference could kill everyone aboard.

The fix airlines and the FAA converged on was mechanical, not attitudinal: the Two-Challenge Rule. If the first officer (or any monitoring pilot) notices a hazardous deviation, they issue a direct verbal challenge. If the captain doesn't respond adequately, they challenge a second time, explicitly. If that second challenge also gets no adequate response, the first officer is now required — not merely permitted — to take control of the aircraft. Removing judgment from the moment of crisis was the point: a junior pilot doesn't have to decide whether defying the captain is justified, only whether two challenges were ignored.

This emerged from the same wave of research that followed United Airlines Flight 173, which crashed near Portland in 1978 after the captain fixated on a landing-gear problem while the crew, aware fuel was critically low, didn't forcefully intervene; ten people died. United built the industry's first formal Crew Resource Management (CRM) program in 1981, growing out of a 1979 NASA workshop on cockpit communication failures. Korean Air became the starkest cautionary case: through the 1990s its crash rate was roughly triple its scale-matched peers (311 fatalities that decade versus 171 at American and 147 at United), culminating in the 1997 Guam crash of Flight 801, where the NTSB cited the first officer's and flight engineer's failure to challenge the captain's approach. Korean Air hired retired Delta VP David Greenberg in 1999, who mandated English-language flying, overhauled training, and dismantled a military-style rank hierarchy; the airline has had no fatal passenger crash since. The Two-Challenge Rule is now standard ICAO/FAA CRM curriculum and has been directly ported into medicine — it's taught at Harvard's Center for Medical Simulation and cited in the Anesthesia Patient Safety Foundation Newsletter as a tool for nurses and junior residents to escalate concerns to attending surgeons.
