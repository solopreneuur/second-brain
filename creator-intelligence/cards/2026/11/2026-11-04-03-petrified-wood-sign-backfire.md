# A sign that tripled wood theft

**The find:** At Arizona's Petrified Forest National Park, researchers planted marked wood and tested anti-theft signs; one truthfully stated "many past visitors have removed petrified wood from the park."
**The surprise:** That honest sign nearly tripled theft, from 2.92% to 7.92%, because it told visitors stealing was common; a sign that just disapproved of the act cut theft to 1.67%.
**The idea:** [Social proof](../../../concepts/social-proof.md) can backfire: describing a bad behaviour as widespread licenses more of it, while stating the approved behaviour suppresses it.

4 · Tiny change, big result · Psychology · [Social Influence, 2006](https://www.fs.usda.gov/psw/publications/winter/psw_2006_winter001.cialdini.pdf)

## In depth

Robert Cialdini, Linda Demaine, Brad Sagarin, Daniel Barrett, Kelton Rhoads, and Patricia Winter published "Managing Social Norms for Persuasive Impact" in Social Influence (2006). The field experiment ran over five consecutive weekends at three sites in Arizona's Petrified Forest National Park, with 2,655 unwitting visitors as subjects. Researchers placed secretly marked pieces of petrified wood on the trails, swapped signs every two-hour block, then counted and replaced the stolen pieces. The park had genuine motivation: officials estimated roughly 14 tons of petrified wood stolen per year, and existing signage already read "Your heritage is being vandalized every day by theft losses of petrified wood of 14 tons a year, mostly a small piece at a time" — a descriptive-norm message the researchers suspected was backfiring.

With no sign (control), 2.92% of marked pieces were stolen. A descriptive-norm sign reading "Many past visitors have removed petrified wood from the park, changing the natural state of the Petrified Forest," paired with an image of three people picking up wood, raised theft to 7.92% — nearly triple the baseline. An injunctive-norm sign reading "Please don't remove the petrified wood from the park, in order to preserve the natural state of the Petrified Forest," paired with an image of a single visitor's hand reaching for wood with a red circle-and-bar over it, cut theft to 1.67%. The design tested descriptive norms ("this is common") against injunctive norms ("this is approved/disapproved"): drawing strong attention to a descriptive norm about bad behavior normalizes it and backfires.

Park administrators initially declined to change the real signage, trusting visitor self-reports over the data — a decision the authors noted with disappointment. Years later, under superintendent Brad Traver, the park did overhaul its messaging: it dropped the "vandalized" framing and now thanks visitors for "doing the right thing." The study sits alongside Cialdini's household-electricity and hotel-towel-reuse norm experiments as core evidence for his theory of normative social influence.
