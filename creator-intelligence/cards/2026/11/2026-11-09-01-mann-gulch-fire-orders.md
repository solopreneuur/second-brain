# A fire that rewrote firefighting rules

**The find:** After 13 smokejumpers died in Montana's 1949 Mann Gulch fire, the US Forest Service reviewed 16 fatal fires from 1937-1956 and wrote the "10 Standard Fire Orders," a strict sequential checklist every US wildfire crew still carries on a card today.
**The surprise:** A sociologist later used the same disaster, independently, as the founding case study for why organizations collapse under pressure — one tragedy producing both a field safety checklist and a management-theory classic.
**The idea:** A [high-reliability organization](../../../concepts/high-reliability-organization.md) survives danger by distributing authority to whoever has the clearest read on the situation, not by adding more top-down rules.

## In depth

On August 5, 1949, a lightning fire in Montana's Mann Gulch grew from a small smoke report at 12:18 pm into a crisis within hours. The Forest Service dropped fifteen smokejumpers near the Missouri River; foreman R. Wagner Dodge led them toward the fire, joined by a sixteenth man, a fire guard already on scene. Around 5:45 pm, as they moved down-gulch, the fire blew up, racing roughly 3,000 acres in about ten minutes, cutting off their route. Dodge did something no one had trained for: he lit a second fire ahead of his own crew, ordered the men to drop their tools and lie down inside its burned-out patch, and survived there while the main fire passed. His crew didn't follow him — the order made no sense to men trained to outrun fire, not lie in one. Thirteen of the sixteen died; only Dodge, Walter Rumsey, and Robert Sallee survived.

In 1957, Forest Service Chief Richard E. McArdle convened a task force under public pressure to stop firefighters from burning to death. It reviewed 16 fatal and near-fatal fires from 1937 to 1956, including Mann Gulch and the 1937 Blackwater fire, and produced the Ten Standard Firefighting Orders — modeled partly on military "General Orders" — a sequential list beginning with "keep informed on fire weather conditions," "know what your fire is doing at all times," and "post lookouts," through to "fight fire aggressively, having provided for safety first." They're still issued on a card to US wildland crews. A later supplement, the "18 Watch Out Situations," flags specific warning signs. At the 1994 South Canyon Fire on Colorado's Storm King Mountain, which killed 14 firefighters, investigators found eight of the ten orders had been compromised and 12 of the 18 situations unheeded.

Separately, in 1993, sociologist Karl Weick used Mann Gulch in *Administrative Science Quarterly* to argue organizations collapse through a "cosmology episode" — when reality stops making sense, roles dissolve, structure vanishes, and panic follows, exactly what happened when Dodge's crew couldn't interpret his escape fire as an order worth obeying.
