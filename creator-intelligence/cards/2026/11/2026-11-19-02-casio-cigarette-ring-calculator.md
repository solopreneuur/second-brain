# Casio's calculator was funded by a cigarette ring

**The find:** In postwar Japan, engineer Toshio Kashio invented the "yubiwa pipe," a cigarette holder mounted on a finger ring so smokers could use every last bit of a scarce, rationed cigarette without burning their fingers. The novelty item became a commercial hit.
**The surprise:** Profits from the cigarette ring, not any electronics expertise or outside investment, funded seven years of unprofitable R&D that produced the Casio 14-A — the world's first compact, all-electric calculator.
**The idea:** A humble, unrelated side product can be the financial bridge that bankrolls the real invention, the same way Dyson's Ballbarrow wheelbarrow funded his years of vacuum prototypes ([Forced detour](../../../concepts/forced-detour.md)).

## In depth

In 1946, Tadao Kashio, the eldest of four brothers, founded a small Tokyo subcontracting workshop in Mitaka making microscope parts and gears. His younger brother Toshio, an engineer who had worked for Japan's Ministry of Communications since age 15 and idolized Thomas Edison, left his job to join the family firm and look for something to invent. Postwar Japan had severe tobacco shortages; smokers routinely smoked unfiltered cigarettes down to the last stub, often burning their fingers in the process. Toshio designed the yubiwa pipe — a cigarette holder mounted on a finger ring — so smokers could use every last bit of a cigarette without discomfort. The product became a genuine commercial hit and gave the family firm its first independent income, letting it move beyond subcontract work.

The brothers split roles clearly: Tadao set strategic direction, Toshio generated ideas, and younger brothers handled sales, manufacturing and engineering drawings for production. In 1949, at Tokyo's first Business Show in Ginza, the brothers saw imported foreign electric calculating machines — expensive, room-sized, and motor-driven. Toshio set out to build a smaller, cheaper, all-electric alternative. The team first built a prototype using solenoids, completed in December 1954 and reportedly Japan's first electric calculator, but solenoids proved mechanically complex and hard to mass-produce. Toshio then redesigned the machine around telephone-exchange-style relays instead.

That work culminated in June 1957 with the Casio 14-A, the world's first compact, all-electric relay calculator, built with 341 relays, capable of all four arithmetic operations to 14 digits, and small enough to sit on a desk rather than fill a room. It used a 10-key numeric pad in the same 4-row, 3-column layout still standard today. Casio Computer Co., Ltd. was formally incorporated that same month. The company went on to release Japan's first calculator with memory in 1965, the breakthrough pocket-sized Casio Mini in 1972, and later expanded into digital watches and keyboards to become a global electronics brand — all traceable back to a ring built for rationed cigarettes.
