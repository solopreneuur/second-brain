# Bing's $100M idea sat ignored

**The find:** A small change to how Bing displayed search-ad headlines was rated low-priority by product managers and sat in the backlog for over six months, until one engineer built it himself in a few days and ran it as an A/B test.
**The surprise:** It turned out to be the single best-performing idea in Bing's history, lifting revenue 12% — over $100 million a year in the US alone.
**The idea:** [Idea-value blindness](../../../concepts/idea-value-blindness.md): expert judgment is a poor predictor of which small ideas matter, which is the case for testing cheaply instead of ranking by committee.

2 · Shocking number · Marketing · [Harvard Business Review](https://hbr.org/2017/09/the-surprising-power-of-online-experiments)

## In depth

In early 2012, someone at Microsoft Bing proposed a small change to how search-ad headlines were displayed: lengthen the ad's title line by merging it with the text of the line immediately below it, so the headline effectively absorbed the first line of ad copy instead of truncating it. It was one of hundreds of feature ideas in the backlog, and Bing's program managers scored it low priority — it looked cosmetic, a "small display tweak," and nothing in prior experience suggested it would move revenue. It sat untouched for more than six months. Eventually a single engineer noticed the implementation would take only a few days of code, decided the idea deserved a real answer rather than a guess, and built and shipped it as a controlled A/B experiment on his own initiative, bypassing the formal prioritization queue entirely.

The result, as told by Ronny Kohavi (then Microsoft's experimentation lead, running the ExP Platform) and Harvard Business School's Stefan Thomke in their HBR piece "The Surprising Power of Online Experiments" (Sept–Oct 2017) and later in Kohavi's book "Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments," was a 12% increase in revenue — translated to over $100 million in incremental annual US revenue — without degrading core user-experience metrics (click-through, query volume, abandonment). It is described as the single best revenue-generating idea in Bing's history. The win was so large it tripped a "too-good-to-be-true" alarm within hours of launch — Kohavi has cited this as an application of Twyman's Law (any figure that looks interesting or surprising is probably wrong), forcing the team to check for bugs or logging errors before trusting the number. It held up.

Kohavi and Thomke use the episode as the article's central lesson: expert judgment is a poor predictor of an idea's actual value, and low-cost-to-test ideas should be tested rather than ranked and shelved by committee. Afterward, Bing leaned further into testing broadly rather than filtering ideas upfront by expected impact. The engineer's name has never been published; no credible source disputes the 12%/$100M figures.
