# Fired, then he built the vacuum

**The find:** Before the vacuum, James Dyson co-founded a real company around the Ballbarrow, a wheelbarrow with a ball instead of a wheel, which hit roughly half of the UK market — then lost control of the patent and was pushed out of his own company in 1979.
**The surprise:** The man famous for refusing to license away his invention had already lost one, because he'd assigned the patent to a company that then fired him.
**The idea:** [Forced detour](../../../concepts/forced-detour.md) — getting pushed out of the "wrong" business can hand you the exact technical problem that becomes the real one.

## In depth

In 1974, James Dyson redesigned the wheelbarrow: a plastic ball instead of a wheel so it wouldn't sink into mud, plus a wide plastic bin instead of a metal tray. He co-founded Kirk-Dyson Designs with financial backer Jeremy Fry to manufacture it, and the Ballbarrow reportedly captured roughly half the UK wheelbarrow market within a few years. Dyson didn't hold the patent personally — like most inventors working through a funded company, he'd assigned it to Kirk-Dyson. When an overseas distribution venture went wrong and the company needed cash, the board, which Dyson didn't control outright, voted to sell off the Ballbarrow rights to raise money, and Dyson was forced out of the company he'd founded in January 1979, walking away with a share of the proceeds but no claim on the product or the patent. While troubleshooting a persistent dust problem in the Ballbarrow factory's spray-finishing room, Dyson had noticed the room used an industrial cyclone tower — a cone-shaped chamber that spins air fast enough to fling dust particles out by centrifugal force, with no filter to clog. It struck him that a vacuum cleaner's main design flaw was its bag: as dust clogs the bag's pores, suction drops steadily throughout use. He spent the next five years and, by his own count, 5,127 prototypes building a bagless vacuum around a cyclone instead of a bag. Every major manufacturer he approached — Hoover, Electrolux, Amway — turned him down, partly because replacement bags were a profitable recurring revenue stream he would be eliminating. He eventually financed and built the vacuum himself, launching the DC01 in the UK in 1993; it became the best-selling vacuum in the country within 18 months, and Dyson Ltd. is now a multi-billion-dollar company.
