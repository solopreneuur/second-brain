# Disney queues hide the line, not the wait

**The find:** When Space Mountain opened in 1975, Disney Imagineer George McGinnis built its queue to switch back through walls on purpose, so a rider can never see more than a short stretch of line ahead, even when the real queue stretches for hundreds of people.
**The surprise:** Disney didn't need to make the wait shorter — hiding how much of it was left was enough to measurably cut how frustrated people felt standing in the exact same line for the exact same amount of time.
**The idea:** Uncertain waits feel longer than known ones, so removing the information that lets you calculate how bad a wait is can matter more than actually shortening it ([Illusion of control](../../../concepts/illusion-of-control.md)).

## In depth

George McGinnis, Space Mountain's Show Concept Designer, pulled the queue, loading area and "SpacePort" inside the building itself when the ride opened at Walt Disney World in January 1975 (Disneyland's version followed in 1977), rather than leaving the line outside in plain view. The path switches back on itself through built walls, so a rider can never see more than a short stretch of the queue ahead, even when the actual line stretches for hundreds of people. McGinnis also folded a planetarium-style pre-show and glimpses of passing vehicles into the queue, turning the wait itself into part of the attraction rather than dead time.

The design encodes decades of queuing research. In his widely cited 1985 essay "The Psychology of Waiting Lines," Harvard Business School's David Maister laid out propositions still referenced today: occupied time feels shorter than unoccupied time, uncertain waits feel longer than known finite ones, unexplained waits feel longer than explained ones, and solo waits feel longer than group waits. Hiding the queue's true length attacks the uncertainty proposition directly — if you cannot see the end, you cannot calculate how bad it is, so dread does not compound with every switchback you clear. Disney reinforces this by posting wait-time estimates padded roughly 20% above the true wait, so guests reliably finish "early" and leave satisfied rather than annoyed.

MIT operations researcher Richard Larson, who studies queuing and is known informally as "Dr. Queue," has pointed to Disney as the industry's master of occupied-time distraction: mosaics, animatronics and story beats such as the Haunted Mansion's stretching room or Rise of the Resistance's holograms that keep attention off the clock rather than off the clock face. Troy Campbell, a former Disney Imagineer with a Duke psychology PhD, later worked on reshaping the story guests tell themselves while waiting rather than rebuilding the physical queue further. Disney later added paid skip-the-line systems — FastPass in 1999, its paid successor Genie+ in 2021 — but the switchback-wall trick predates both by decades, and variants of serpentine, single-file queuing that block line-of-sight were independently adopted by banks and airports once the same psychology was shown to generalize beyond theme parks.
