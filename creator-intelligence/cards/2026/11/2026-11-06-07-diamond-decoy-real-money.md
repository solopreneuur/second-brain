# A worse diamond sold the real one

**The find:** Researchers studying an online diamond retailer's real sales found that when a deliberately inferior "decoy" diamond sat next to a target diamond, buyers chose the target 1.8 to 3.2 times more often, lifting the retailer's gross profit 14.3% — even though shoppers consciously noticed the decoy only 11 to 25% of the time.
**The surprise:** Nearly every decoy-effect study before this used hypothetical lab choices; this one moved real money, and it worked even bigger than the lab versions.
**The idea:** The [decoy effect](../../../concepts/decoy-effect.md): a dominated option makes the target look better by comparison, even when barely noticed.

## In depth

The study is Chunhua Wu and Koray Cosguner, "Profiting from the Decoy Effect: A Case Study of an Online Diamond Retailer," Marketing Science, Vol. 39, No. 5 (2020), pp. 974-995. The retailer itself is not named in the paper; it's described only as a leading online diamond marketplace. The data came from daily web-crawler snapshots of the retailer's listings from February to September 2011, covering round-cut diamonds priced between $2,000 and $20,000 — over 2.7 million diamond-day observations in total.

Rather than running a lab experiment, Wu and Cosguner mined naturally occurring decoy relationships out of the listings themselves. For any two diamonds, they flagged one as a "decoy" dominated by a "target" diamond if the decoy was worse on at least one of the 4Cs (carat, cut, color, clarity) and no better on any of them, while costing the same or more — or if the two had identical 4Cs but the decoy was priced at least 5% higher. Every day, this produced thousands of dominant/decoy pairs sitting side by side in search results, effectively a huge uncontrolled field experiment with genuine money on the line.

They built a diamond-level proportional hazard model splitting a diamond's daily probability of selling into two pieces: whether shoppers noticed the decoy relationship at all, and how much the sale hazard jumped once they did. Detection probability — essentially, consciously registering that one stone was strictly worse than another nearby stone — was low, 11% to 25% depending on price segment. But when shoppers did detect it, the dominant diamond's hazard of selling that day rose 1.8 to 3.2 times. Aggregated across the full inventory, this lifted the retailer's gross profit by 14.3%.

This mattered because the decoy effect — first demonstrated by Huber, Payne, and Puto in 1982 using hypothetical choices among beers and cars — had been replicated dozens of times but almost always in surveys or lab tasks with imaginary stakes. Wu and Cosguner's paper was among the first to confirm it moves real purchases and real profit with actual consumers' own money.
