# An Elevator Patent Waited 60 Years

**The find:** "Destination dispatch" elevators — type your floor in the lobby, get assigned a specific car — were patented in 1961 by Sydney engineer Leo Port, decades before cheap microprocessors existed to run the allocation math; the patent lapsed in 1977 before anyone had commercialized it.
**The surprise:** The "smart elevator" behavior that still confuses first-time riders, who wonder why it sent their friend to a different car, is over 60 years old and was blocked by hardware, not insight.
**The idea:** [Right idea, wrong time](../../../concepts/right-idea-wrong-time.md): an idea can be fully invented and patented, and still sit unused for decades purely because its dependent technology hasn't caught up.

## In depth

Leo Port was a Sydney engineer — later the city's Lord Mayor — who in 1961 conceived and patented what he called "Port-El," a system that grouped elevator passengers heading to the same floor into the same car, assigned by an algorithm the moment they entered their destination on a lobby keypad, rather than the traditional method of calling any available car and pressing a floor button once inside. The idea was sound: Schindler's own later marketing materials note destination dispatch can cut overall trip times by roughly 25% and raise effective capacity by about 30%, by cutting down on wasted intermediate stops.

The problem was entirely mechanical. In 1961, elevator controllers ran on banks of electromechanical relays, which simply couldn't execute the kind of dynamic, combinatorial allocation Port's system needed — assigning every rider's destination against every other rider's destination and every car's current position, in real time. Port's system saw limited real-world use anyway, installed at the University of Sydney's Law School and the Australian Milk Marketing Board's offices, but it never spread, and Port allowed the patent to lapse in 1977 without licensing it further.

Microprocessor-controlled elevators capable of running the necessary logic didn't become practical until the late 1970s and widespread until the 1980s. Schindler Group's engineers Joris Schröder and Paul Friedli built on that hardware to launch Miconic 10 in 1990 — generally credited as the first commercially successful destination dispatch system, installed first at Hamburg Electric Company in Germany — almost thirty years after Port's original patent. Rival systems followed: Mitsubishi's DOAS-S in 2002, then Otis's Compass and Kone's Polaris in 2005. Schindler has since iterated through SchindlerID, then PORT, then myPORT, each generation refining the same core idea Port described in 1961 — he's credited by the industry as the first to propose it, even though the product that finally shipped bore his name only by coincidence.
