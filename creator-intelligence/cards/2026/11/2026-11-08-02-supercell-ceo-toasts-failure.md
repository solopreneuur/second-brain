# Supercell's CEO Toasts His Own Failure

**The find:** Supercell is famous for popping champagne when a game dies, to destigmatize failure for the small "cell" teams that built it — so when CEO Ilkka Paananen decided in 2023 to break the company's winning flat structure, which almost every advisor told him not to touch, he stood in front of 500+ employees and toasted his own leadership failure before making the change.
**The surprise:** A ritual built to forgive junior teams got turned on the CEO himself, in public, as the actual mechanism for forcing through an unpopular structural change.
**The idea:** [Radical honesty](../../../concepts/radical-honesty.md): naming your own failure out loud, before anyone can accuse you of it, is what makes people accept a change leadership alone could never sell by argument.

## In depth

Supercell, founded in Helsinki in 2010, built Clash of Clans, Clash Royale, Hay Day, Brawl Stars and Boom Beach by organizing developers into small, fully autonomous "cells" — the org chart flipped upside down, with the CEO explicitly serving the cells rather than directing them. The model generated close to $2 billion a year in revenue, with Clash of Clans alone grossing over $10 billion since launch, and it came with a signature culture: when a cell killed a failed game, the team toasted the decision with champagne, celebrating the learning rather than mourning the loss.

By 2023 the formula had stalled — five years without a new global hit — and Harvard Business School's "Supercell 2.0: Clash of Plans" case, discussed on HBR's Cold Call podcast with senior lecturer Jeffrey Rayport, lays out what CEO Ilkka Paananen did next. He and his leadership team split the company into two divisions: New Games, which kept the small, flat, autonomous cells for fresh ideas, and Live Games, which added real hierarchy and structure to systematically exploit the five existing franchises. Advisors warned him not to touch a system that was printing money. Paananen's response was to gather more than 500 employees, bring out a bottle of champagne, and toast a failure — his own, as CEO, for not making the organizational change sooner. Rayport frames the underlying problem as a classic exploration-versus-exploitation conflict: the cells had been asked to do both jobs — invent new hits and milk existing ones — with the same structure, which guaranteed neither would be done as well as it could be.

The reorg's commercial verdict came fast: profitability rose more than 80%, and revenue grew from just over $2 billion to over $3 billion and held there into the following year. Whether the New Games side will produce more hits of its own is, as Rayport notes, still an open question.
