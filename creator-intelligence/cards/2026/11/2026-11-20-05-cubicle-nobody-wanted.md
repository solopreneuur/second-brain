# The cubicle's inventor hated cubicles

**The find:** Designer Robert Propst invented the cubicle in 1968 as "Action Office II" — angled, modular panels meant to give office workers privacy and freedom of movement, explicitly not meant to lock into rigid 90-degree boxes. Furniture makers mass-produced it as square cubes anyway, because right angles were cheaper to manufacture and fit into a floor plan.
**The surprise:** Propst spent his later life publicly disowning his own invention, calling the mass-produced result "monolithic insanity" — the thing everyone associates with his name is the opposite of what he designed.
**The idea:** [Implementation drift](../../../concepts/implementation-drift.md) — a design's intent gets overwritten by whoever manufactures and scales it, until the scaled version becomes "the design" in collective memory.

## In depth

Robert Propst was a sculptor and inventor working for furniture maker Herman Miller in the 1960s when he was asked to rethink the American office, which at the time largely consisted of open rows of identical desks — the "bullpen" model inherited from early-20th-century factory floors. Propst spent years studying how people actually worked: he observed that knowledge workers needed to shift between standing and sitting, needed varied surfaces at different heights, and needed some visual privacy without being sealed into a box. His 1968 design, "Action Office II," used angled panels — deliberately not meeted at 90 degrees — so a workstation could flex to the task and the person, creating an irregular, almost organic floor plan rather than a grid.

The problem was economic, not conceptual. Angled panels are expensive and space-inefficient to manufacture and arrange; square panels snap together in a simple repeating grid that a facilities manager can tile across a floor plate with no waste and no customization. Herman Miller and its competitors quickly began selling simplified versions of Action Office using 90-degree corners, and within a decade "the cubicle" — rows of identical rectangular boxes, as many as could be crammed per square foot — had become the default American office, driven not by Propst's research but by real-estate economics: square footage per employee was the metric that mattered to the people buying the furniture, not the worker's experience inside it.

Propst watched this happen and was horrified. In interviews before his death in 2000, he explicitly repudiated the result, telling people the cubicle farms bearing almost no resemblance to his original intent were, in his words, "monolithic insanity" — he had tried to design more human office space and had instead, through no fault of his own design, given corporate America a tool for packing workers as densely as cheaply as possible. The cubicle remains one of the starkest examples of a now-common pattern: the version of an invention that survives contact with mass manufacturing and real-estate economics is rarely the version its inventor actually designed, and the inventor often has no leverage to stop the drift once it starts.
