# Berkshire Hathaway's name outlived its mills

**The find:** Berkshire Hathaway was a failing New England textile manufacturer. Warren Buffett began buying its stock in 1962 as a classic "cigar butt" cheap-asset bet, took control in 1965, and kept the mills running at a loss for two decades before finally shutting the last one down in July 1985.
**The surprise:** He kept only the corporate shell — the name of a company he spent twenty years deliberately winding down is now the name of one of the most valuable companies on Earth.
**The idea:** [Shell company survivorship](../../../concepts/shell-company-survivorship.md) — a famous brand name can outlive, by decades, the actual business that built it.

## In depth

Berkshire Hathaway began as two New England textile firms: Hathaway Manufacturing, founded in New Bedford, Massachusetts in 1888, and Berkshire Fine Spinning Associates, a rollup of older Massachusetts and Rhode Island cotton mills. The two merged in 1955 to form Berkshire Hathaway, briefly a 15-plant, 12,000-worker operation generating over $120 million in revenue. By then New England textiles were already losing to cheaper Southern and foreign mills, and seven of the 15 plants were gone within a few years.

Warren Buffett started buying the stock in 1962 at around $7.50 a share, a classic Ben Graham "cigar butt": a company selling below liquidation value that he expected to profit from as it closed plants and bought back stock. In 1964, Berkshire's president, Seabury Stanton, verbally agreed to tender for Buffett's shares at $11.50. The written offer came in at $11 3/8 — an eighth of a point lower. Buffett, angered by the chiseling, instead bought more stock and seized control, pushing Stanton and his son out in 1965. Buffett later called this "the dumbest stock I ever bought," estimating in his 2014 letter that funneling his career through a dying textile maker rather than starting an insurer from scratch cost shareholders roughly $200 billion in forgone compounding.

For the next two decades Buffett kept the mills running, using their cash flow and tax losses to buy National Indemnity in 1967 and build the insurance-and-securities engine that became modern Berkshire. In his 1978 letter he gave four reasons for not shutting textiles down, including that the mills were major local employers; by 1985 he admitted he'd been wrong that the business could still earn adequate cash returns. In July 1985 Berkshire closed its last textile operations, in New Bedford, idling roughly 400 workers. The equipment, which had cost about $13 million, sold at auction for gross proceeds of $163,122 — modern looms bought for $5,000 apiece in 1981 fetched $26 each as scrap. Buffett's lesson, repeated in later letters: "a good managerial record is far more a function of what business boat you get into than it is of how effectively you row."
