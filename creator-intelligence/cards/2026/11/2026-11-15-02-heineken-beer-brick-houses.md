# Heineken's beer bottle bricks

**The find:** In 1963 Heineken had architect John Habraken design the WOBO, a beer bottle shaped like an interlocking brick so that, once emptied, it could be mortared straight into a wall; a trial run made 100,000 of them and a prototype house went up near Heineken's own estate.
**The surprise:** The project wasn't killed by an engineering failure — Heineken's own marketing department objected that a brick-shaped bottle for a premium beer would look bad next to housing built for the poor.
**The idea:** A product that solves the problem it was built for can still die to protect the brand's image.

## In depth

In 1960, Heineken chairman Alfred "Freddy" Heineken toured Curaçao and saw two things side by side: beaches littered with empty bottles and acute housing shortages. He asked Dutch architect N. John Habraken, then in his early thirties, to design "a brick that holds beer." Habraken's first version — two bulbous compartments under a long shaft — was rejected by Heineken's own marketing department as "effeminate." He revised it into a squared-off bottle with a short neck recessed into the base and a grid of raised bumps on its flat sides, so mortar could grip it like a real brick. The bottle came in two sizes, 35cl and 50cl, mirroring the half-brick-and-full-brick offset needed to stagger and bond courses in a wall. Royal Leerdam's Vereenigde Glasfabrieken produced a trial run of 100,000 WOBOs ("World Bottles") in 1963–64. Roughly 1,000 bottles, laid flat and mortared, could build a hut about three meters square. In 1965 a prototype glass house went up near Heineken's own villa in Noordwijk; it was later demolished, though at least one small WOBO shed and a converted garage reportedly survived on the estate for decades.

The project died anyway. The most detailed sourced account puts marketing objection as the primary cause: executives worried about brand damage, framing it bluntly as how it would look if poor people built houses out of a premium beer bottle. Liability fears and practical costs compounded it — the bottles needed thicker, heavier glass to bear structural load, making them pricier and slower to mold than standard stock, and corners were prone to chipping. About 60,000 unused bottles sat in a Rotterdam warehouse afterward. Habraken went on to write the influential 1961 manifesto "Supports: An Alternative to Mass Housing," founded SAR in the Netherlands, and later taught at MIT, building the "open building" theory that separates permanent structural "supports" from resident-customizable "infill" — a framework still taught in housing design today. Surviving WOBOs sit in the V&A's collection and Het Nieuwe Instituut; the concept is widely cited as a forerunner to modern "ecobrick" construction used in disaster-relief housing.
