# Square's patent skips its founders

**The find:** Square's iconic card-reader dongle patent (US 7,810,729) lists neither Jack Dorsey nor Jim McKelvey as inventor — only Robert Morley, a Washington University professor and family friend who built the actual hardware, assigned to his own holding company, not Square.
**The surprise:** Morley sued in 2014 saying he'd been cut out of the company he helped invent; Square countersued over McKelvey being left off the patent; it ended in a $50 million settlement in 2016.
**The idea:** A company's founding myth and its legal paper trail are often two different stories ([Narrative fallacy](../../../concepts/narrative-fallacy.md)) — credit on the page and credit in the story don't have to match.

## In depth

Jim McKelvey lost a sale in his glassblowing studio in 2009 because he couldn't take a credit card, and the story goes that he and Jack Dorsey sketched the idea for a tiny card-reader dongle that plugs into a phone's headphone jack. The actual engineering — the audio-jack swipe mechanism that became Square's signature hardware — was built by Robert Morley, a professor at Washington University in St. Louis and a longtime family friend of McKelvey's. When the core patent (US 7,810,729, "Method for conducting financial transactions") was filed, it named Morley alone as inventor, assigned not to Square but to a separate holding company Morley controlled.

For several years that didn't matter — Square licensed the technology and grew into a payments giant. But the relationship frayed. In 2014, Morley sued Square and McKelvey, alleging he'd been squeezed out of equity and recognition in the company his invention had made possible, despite being treated, by his account, as an unpaid long-term contributor rather than a true co-founder. Square countersued, arguing in part that McKelvey himself should have been named as a co-inventor on Morley's patent and had been improperly excluded. The dispute dragged through litigation until a 2016 settlement reported at roughly $50 million — a sum larger than Square's quarterly losses at the time — closing the case without a public trial record of exactly who contributed what.

The patent record is a clean illustration of how origin stories compress: the "two guys and an idea" founding narrative that gets told in pitch decks and press coverage doesn't necessarily match who the patent office, or the courts, credit as the actual inventor. The legal paper trail (who's named on a filing, who holds the assignment) and the public narrative (who's remembered as the founder) can diverge sharply, and the gap only becomes visible when money is large enough to make someone sue over it.
