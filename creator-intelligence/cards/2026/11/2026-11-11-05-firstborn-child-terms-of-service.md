# Nearly everyone signed away a firstborn child

**The find:** Researchers had 543 people sign up for a fake social network by agreeing to its terms of service. Buried in the fine print: users would pay with their firstborn child and share their data with the NSA and their employer.
**The surprise:** 98% missed the clause entirely, and 93% agreed to the terms anyway — most people spent under a minute on documents that would take over 40 minutes to actually read.
**The idea:** [Terms of service blindness](../../../concepts/terms-of-service-blindness.md) — a written agreement can be functionally invisible even when the stakes it names are absurd.

2 · Shocking number · Psychology · [Obar & Oeldorf-Hirsch, Information, Communication & Society](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1369118X.2018.1486870)

## In depth

In 2014/2015, communication researchers Jonathan A. Obar (then at Michigan State University, later York University in Toronto) and Anne Oeldorf-Hirsch (University of Connecticut) ran an experiment to measure how many people actually read the legal agreements they click "I agree" to. They built a fake social networking service called NameDrop, modeled visually and textually on real platforms, and recruited 543 undergraduate students to sign up for it as a class exercise. Signup required agreeing to a privacy policy and a terms-of-service document, together running roughly 12,000 words. Buried in the text were two "gotcha" clauses: one stated that user data would be shared with the NSA and with the user's employer, and another stated that users were giving up their firstborn child as payment — with a clause specifying that if the user didn't yet have a child, the service would still claim the first one born before 2050. A "quick join" clickwrap option let participants skip straight to checking the boxes without opening either document.

74% of participants chose quick join and skipped the privacy policy entirely; of those who did open the documents, average time spent was 73 seconds on the privacy policy and 51 seconds on the terms of service — compared to an estimated 30 minutes and 16 minutes respectively to actually read them at normal reading speed. 98% of all participants missed the gotcha clauses. 93% agreed to the terms of service and roughly 97% agreed to the privacy policy. Participants who declined only spent about 30 to 90 seconds longer on the documents than those who accepted — not enough extra time to have read them either. The paper, titled "The Biggest Lie on the Internet," was first covered in the press around July 2016 and formally published in Information, Communication & Society in 2020. A 2022 follow-up by the same authors tested 500 adults aged 50+ with a revised clause requiring a kidney as payment, finding similarly high rates of blind agreement. The findings echo earlier real-world stunts: UK retailer GameStation's 2010 April Fools' clause claimed customers' "immortal souls" (only 12% opted out), and PC Pitstop's 2004 EULA buried a $1,000 reward for reading it — unclaimed for over four months and 3,000 downloads.
