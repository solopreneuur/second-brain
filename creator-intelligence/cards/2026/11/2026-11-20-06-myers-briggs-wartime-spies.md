# Myers-Briggs' first buyer was wartime spies

**The find:** Isabel Briggs Myers built her personality test while apprenticing at a Philadelphia personnel firm, but its first real-world buyer wasn't a corporation — it was Donald MacKinnon's "Station S," the OSS's secret World War II assessment center, which used it to decide who was psychologically fit to be dropped behind enemy lines as a spy.
**The surprise:** The test now used by HR departments to sort office workers into friendly "types" began its working life helping intelligence officers pick candidates for espionage missions where failure meant capture or death.
**The idea:** A [repurposed signal](../../../concepts/repurposed-signal.md) — a measurement tool built for a low-stakes context can get adopted for a radically higher-stakes one long before it reaches its eventual mass use.

## In depth

Isabel Briggs Myers had no formal training in psychology — she was a homemaker and writer who became fascinated by Carl Jung's theory of psychological types after reading his 1921 book, and spent years informally refining a questionnaire with her mother, Katharine Cook Briggs, to sort people into Jung's categories. In the early 1940s, Myers took a research position at a Philadelphia-area personnel consulting firm, Edward N. Hay & Associates, where she had access to real hiring data and began validating and expanding her questionnaire against it — this period is where the modern Myers-Briggs Type Indicator took real shape.

The test's first serious institutional validation, however, came from an unexpected direction. During World War II, the Office of Strategic Services — the US intelligence agency that preceded the CIA — set up a secret assessment center near Washington, DC, known internally as "Station S," run by psychologist Donald MacKinnon. Its job was to evaluate candidates being considered for extremely high-risk clandestine operations: infiltrating occupied territory, running sabotage missions, operating undercover behind enemy lines. MacKinnon's team used an array of psychological assessment tools to try to predict which candidates had the specific combination of traits needed to survive sustained deception and isolation under mortal threat — and personality-typing instruments like the one Myers was developing became part of that toolkit, used to try to screen out people whose psychological profile made them unsuited for the stress of espionage.

This is a strange prehistory for a test now mostly associated with HR icebreakers, team-building workshops, and dating-app bios. Myers continued refining and marketing the test after the war, eventually publishing the formal Myers-Briggs Type Indicator in 1962 with the help of Educational Testing Service, and it has gone on to become one of the most widely used — and, among academic psychologists, most widely criticized for weak reliability and validity — personality instruments in the world, taken by an estimated two million people a year. Merve Emre's 2018 book "The Personality Brokers" traces this full arc, from Jung's typology through the mother-daughter amateur psychologists to OSS spy vetting to corporate HR, and argues the instrument's scientific pedigree has always been far shakier than its institutional reach would suggest.
