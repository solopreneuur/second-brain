# The White House's exiled solar panels

**The find:** Jimmy Carter put 32 solar water-heating panels on the White House roof in 1979. Reagan had them quietly removed in 1986, and they sat in a warehouse until a Maine college bought a batch for $500 and drove them home in a soccer-team bus.
**The surprise:** When activists later tried to return one original panel to the roof as a symbolic gesture, the Obama White House declined — reportedly because the object still carried "an association with failure" decades after the policy that killed it was gone.
**The idea:** A symbol can absorb the stigma of the decision that ended it, and keep radiating that stigma long after the decision itself stops mattering ([Symbolic contamination](../../../concepts/symbolic-contamination.md)).

## In depth

In June 1979, President Jimmy Carter had 32 solar panels installed on the roof of the White House West Wing, above the staff kitchen. They weren't photovoltaic cells but solar hot-water panels — glazed collectors that used sunlight to heat water, providing roughly 75 percent of the energy needed to heat about 1,000 gallons of water a day. Carter framed the installation as a statement about the energy crisis, reportedly saying that whether the panels became "a curiosity, a museum piece, an example of a road not taken" or part of "one of the greatest adventures ever undertaken by the American people" was up to the country. Ronald Reagan, whose energy policy favored fossil fuel production over conservation, had the panels quietly taken down in 1986 during roof repairs, with no public announcement. They went into storage in a federal warehouse.

In 1991, Peter Marbach, a development officer at Unity College in Maine, read about the panels, contacted the General Services Administration, and wrote to Carter himself, who sent back a handwritten note of encouragement. Marbach then bought a batch of the panels for about $500 and drove to Washington, D.C. in the college's soccer-team bus to haul them back to Maine. Sixteen were refurbished and mounted on the roof of Unity's cafeteria, where they heated water for more than a decade, until roughly 2010. Of the rest, individual panels were donated to the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History, the Carter Presidential Library, the Solar Energy Industries Association, and Himin Solar Energy Group's "Sun-Moon Mansion" museum near Beijing; one went on loan to the U.S. Department of Energy. Unity retained most of the original 32.

In September 2010, climate activist Bill McKibben and a group of Unity College students drove one of the original panels to Washington in a biodiesel-fueled van, hoping Obama's staff would symbolically reinstall it on the White House roof. The administration declined, continuing what officials called a "deliberative process," and commentary at the time suggested the panels were seen as carrying "an association with failure" — a politically risky artifact of a defeated energy agenda rather than a usable technology. Obama did eventually add new solar panels to the White House in 2014, but not Carter's originals.
