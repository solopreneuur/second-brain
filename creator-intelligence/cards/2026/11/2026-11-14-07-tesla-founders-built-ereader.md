# Tesla's founders built an e-reader first

**The find:** Martin Eberhard and Marc Tarpenning built the Rocket eBook in 1998, a $499 e-reader with a 40-hour battery, nearly a decade before Kindle; they sold the company to Gemstar for ~$187M, watched Gemstar kill the product line, then founded Tesla Motors.
**The surprise:** The people who built Tesla's first car weren't car people at all — they were failed e-reader makers who had spent years wrestling with battery limits on a small device.
**The idea:** A failure in one category can hand founders the exact constraint-solving skill that becomes a breakthrough somewhere else entirely ([constraint-driven skill transfer](../../../concepts/constraint-skill-transfer.md)).

## In depth

In 1998, Martin Eberhard and Marc Tarpenning's startup NuvoMedia launched the Rocket eBook, one of the first dedicated e-readers — a $499 device with a monochrome LCD screen, room for about ten books, and roughly 20-40 hours of battery life depending on backlight use. In late 1997 the two founders and their lawyer flew to Seattle and spent three weeks pitching the device to Jeff Bezos. Bezos was interested but wanted exclusivity and veto power over future investors; NuvoMedia refused, partly because Bezos admitted he worried that helping them succeed might just let Barnes & Noble buy the company out from under him later. NuvoMedia partnered with Barnes & Noble instead. In February 2000, NuvoMedia sold to Gemstar-TV Guide International for about $187 million in stock. Gemstar merged it with rival SoftBook, rebadged the hardware as the RCA eBook (REB1100, $299), and ran it into the ground — it stopped selling e-book readers in June 2003 and stopped selling e-books the following month, nearly four years before Amazon's Kindle launched in November 2007.

The real carryover wasn't the reading device — it was the batteries. Building the Rocket eBook, Eberhard and Tarpenning had sourced Panasonic 18650 lithium-ion cells, the same cylindrical cells used in laptops, because they packed far more energy density than NiMH despite being "finicky," as Tarpenning later put it. When they founded Tesla Motors in July 2003 — Eberhard as CEO, Tarpenning as CFO/VP of engineering — they applied the identical logic at automotive scale: rather than engineer exotic new battery chemistry, Tesla's Roadster used roughly 6,800 of those same commodity 18650 cells, networked together, reasoning that the world's highest energy-density batteries were already being mass-produced by the billions. Elon Musk led a $6.5 million Series A in February 2004 and became chairman. Eberhard was pushed out as CEO in August 2007, sued Musk in June 2009 for libel and breach of contract, then dropped the suit weeks later; the September 2009 settlement let all five — Eberhard, Tarpenning, Ian Wright, Musk, and JB Straubel — officially call themselves Tesla co-founders.
