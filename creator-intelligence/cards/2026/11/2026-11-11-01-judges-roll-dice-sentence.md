# Judges roll dice before sentencing

**The find:** German trial judges with an average of 15+ years' experience rolled a pair of loaded dice that always landed on 3 or 9, then recommended a prison sentence for a shoplifter in a case vignette. Judges who rolled 9 recommended an average of 8 months; judges who rolled 3 recommended an average of 5 months.
**The surprise:** The judges knew the dice were random, had rolled them with their own hands, and still let the number move their sentence by roughly 50%.
**The idea:** [Anchoring](../../../concepts/anchoring.md) — an irrelevant number, once stated, pulls a judgment toward it even when the person doing the judging knows it's meaningless.

## In depth

In 2006, psychologists Birte Englich, Thomas Mussweiler, and Fritz Strack published "Playing Dice With Criminal Sentences" in Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin (vol. 32, pp. 188-200), part of a series of studies testing whether legal experts resist irrelevant numerical anchors the way intuition says they should. In the dice experiment, German trial judges with an average of more than 15 years on the bench read a vignette about a woman caught shoplifting. Before sentencing her, each judge rolled a pair of dice that looked ordinary but were loaded to stop on a sum of either 3 or 9 — never anything else. After the roll, judges were asked whether their sentence would be higher or lower than the number on the dice, then asked to name an exact sentence in months. Judges who rolled 9 recommended an average of 8 months in prison; judges who rolled 3 recommended an average of 5 months — a roughly 50% swing driven by a number everyone present knew was random, generated by the judges' own hands, with no bearing on the case. The same paper reports companion studies using other irrelevant anchors on legal experts: a journalist's offhand sentencing suggestion, and a sentencing demand attributed to a prosecutor who was actually a computer science student with no legal standing — both anchors moved sentences the same way. A separate strand of the researchers' work (Englich & Mussweiler 2001; Englich, Mussweiler & Strack 2005) showed real prosecutors' own sentencing demands anchor the sentences judges and even defense attorneys later settle on, and found that a week's delay between anchor and decision did not weaken the pull. Across this body of work, more experience and expertise did not reduce the bias; the researchers argue experts may be just as susceptible because expertise gives them more consistent knowledge to retrieve in the anchor's favor. The mechanism offered is Strack and Mussweiler's selective accessibility model: an anchor is tested like a hypothesis, which selectively activates anchor-consistent facts and associations, which then dominate the final judgment because they come to mind more easily than anchor-inconsistent ones. Anchoring is considered one of the better-replicating findings from that era of social psychology — the 36-country Many Labs 2 project reproduced it at effect sizes as large as or larger than the original reports — though the original judge-sample sizes were modest.
