# Your legs don't believe the escalator is off

**The find:** Step onto a broken escalator you know perfectly well is switched off, and your body still lurches forward and fires the same leg muscles it would need on a moving one — a named effect called the broken escalator phenomenon.
**The surprise:** People can't turn this off even when they try. Knowing the facts and controlling your own body are two separate systems, and in this case the knowing one loses every time.
**The idea:** Conscious belief and automatic motor memory run on separate tracks, and the automatic one doesn't update just because you've been told the truth ([Broken escalator phenomenon](../../../concepts/broken-escalator-phenomenon.md)).

## In depth

In 2003, Raymond Reynolds and Adolfo Bronstein at Imperial College London published "The broken escalator phenomenon: aftereffect of walking onto a moving platform" in Experimental Brain Research. They tested 14 healthy subjects on a sled rigged to move like an escalator. Subjects first walked onto the sled while it was stationary (baseline), then walked onto it 20 times while it moved forward, during which their approach walking speed rose from a baseline of about 0.60 m/s to about 0.90 m/s as they adjusted to the motion. Then, with the sled switched off and subjects fully informed it would not move, they walked onto it again. Despite knowing it was stationary, subjects walked onto the dead platform at an inappropriately fast 0.71 m/s, their torsos lurched forward in a pronounced overshoot of sway, and EMG recordings from ankle flexor and extensor muscles showed elevated leg muscle activity — all matching the motor pattern needed to counteract a moving surface, not a stationary one. Subjects reported genuine surprise at losing balance, and critically, the effect persisted even when they deliberately tried to suppress it, demonstrating a split between declarative knowledge ("this isn't moving") and procedural motor memory (the body still executes the adaptation).

Follow-up work from the same lab extended the finding. Research tested patients lacking vestibular (labyrinthine) function and found the inner ear does not act as a brake on the aftereffect, indicating the adaptation lives elsewhere in the motor/locomotor control system rather than in balance organs themselves. Later studies examined how trial number and approach velocity shape the size of the aftereffect, and a 2013 study characterized it as a "pre-emptive" postural response already detectable before the foot even contacts the platform. In 2020, Mitesh Patel, Ed Roberts, Qadeer Arshad, Karen Bunday, John Golding, Diego Kaski, and Bronstein published a study showing that patients with vestibular dizziness, including Meniere's disease, display an exaggerated version of the broken-escalator sway and reduced ability to adapt, with aftereffect size tracking symptom severity — positioning the paradigm as a possible tool for probing locomotor adaptation deficits in balance disorders and fall risk.
