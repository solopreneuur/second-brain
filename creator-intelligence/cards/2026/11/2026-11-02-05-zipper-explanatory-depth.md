# You can't actually explain a zipper

**The find:** Yale researchers asked people to rate how well they understood a zipper, toilet or cylinder lock, then made them write a step-by-step explanation. Confidence collapsed across 12 studies.
**The surprise:** People don't forget how a zipper works between the rating and the explanation — they never knew. Familiarity was quietly standing in for understanding the whole time.
**The idea:** [Illusion of explanatory depth](../../../concepts/illusion-of-explanatory-depth.md): mistaking familiarity for mechanistic understanding, strongest for how things work rather than facts or stories.

9 · Unfamiliar concept · Psychology · [Rozenblit & Keil, Cognitive Science (2002)](https://cogdevlab.yale.edu/sites/default/files/files/rozenblit%20&%20keil%20%202002.pdf)

## In depth

Yale researchers Leonid Rozenblit and Frank Keil ran a series of 12 studies, published in Cognitive Science in 2002 as "The Misunderstood Limits of Folk Science: An Illusion of Explanatory Depth." The core procedure: give people everyday mechanical objects — a zipper, a flush toilet, a cylinder lock, a speedometer, a sewing machine, a piano key, a quartz watch, a helicopter — and ask them to rate, on a 7-point scale, how well they understand the mechanism that makes each one work, from a bare, naive grasp to deep, expert-level insight. Then ask them to actually write out, step by step, a causal explanation of how the device works internally. Then ask them to rate their understanding again.

Ratings dropped sharply after people tried to write the explanation. Confronted with the gaps in their own account — unable to specify, say, exactly how a cylinder lock's pins release or how a zipper's slider interlocks teeth — participants consistently and substantially revised their confidence downward. The effect was specific to causal, mechanistic knowledge: when Rozenblit and Keil ran parallel studies using facts, procedures, narratives (movie plots), and dance steps instead of device mechanisms, the confidence drop after explanation was much smaller or absent, because people's initial confidence in those domains was already better calibrated to what they actually knew. They called the mechanism-specific gap the "illusion of explanatory depth" — the sense that we understand how things work in far more detail than we can actually produce on demand.

The idea has been extended well beyond gadgets. A widely cited 2013 Psychological Science study by Philip Fernbach, Todd Rogers, Craig Fox, and Steven Sloman, "Political Extremism Is Supported by an Illusion of Understanding," applied the same logic to policy opinions. Participants with strong views on issues like cap-and-trade or a flat tax were asked either to list reasons for their position, or to explain, mechanistically, step by step, how the policy would actually produce its effects. Listing reasons didn't change anything. Writing the mechanistic explanation did: people holding the most extreme positions moderated them afterward, and were less willing to donate money to an advocacy group for the cause.

## Deeper · 2026-11-02

The illusion turns out to have a predecessor that actually beat Rozenblit and Keil into print on a narrower claim. Rebecca Lawson, a psychologist at the University of Liverpool, published "The Science of Cycology: Failures to Understand How Everyday Objects Work" in Memory & Cognition in 2006, using a test object almost everyone in her sample used daily: a bicycle. She gave students a drawing of a bicycle frame with the chain, pedals, and some frame segments missing and asked them to complete it. Over half got it wrong in basic, mechanically fatal ways — drawing the chain looping around the front wheel as well as the back, or attaching pedals directly to the front wheel so the bike could never actually move. These were people who rode bicycles regularly and rated their own understanding as solid beforehand. Lawson's twist mattered: unlike Rozenblit and Keil's self-generated written explanations, her task had an objectively checkable right answer, which meant the failures couldn't be waved away as a quirk of self-report. Letting people see a real bicycle to copy, or simply having more cycling experience, improved performance but never eliminated the errors — gestural, procedural knowledge (how to ride) stayed walled off from causal, mechanistic knowledge (how the machine works).

The effect has since been pushed, stretched, and in one prominent case, partly broken. A 2024 paper by Ethan Meyers, Jeremy Gretton, Joshua Budge, Jonathan Fugelsang, and Derek Koehler at the University of Waterloo, "Broad Effects of Shallow Understanding," ran three preregistered Mechanical Turk experiments (240, 327, and 264 participants) and found something Rozenblit and Keil's framework didn't predict: explaining one device drags down people's confidence about unrelated devices too. Participants who wrote out how a helicopter works rated their understanding of zippers lower afterward, even though they'd explained nothing about zippers. That result pushes against the "specificity principle" baked into the original study — the idea that explaining X should only expose gaps in X — and suggests the illusion partly operates as a general, domain-spanning overconfidence that gets punctured by the mere act of generating any detailed causal account. Meanwhile, the signature extension of the idea — Fernbach et al.'s claim that explaining policy mechanisms moderates political extremism — has had a rough decade. Jarret Crawford and John Ruscio ran preregistered replications (two of Experiment 2, with 306 and 405 participants, plus one of Experiment 3 with 343) and found the core metacognitive piece held — people's self-rated understanding dropped exactly as before — but the political moderation effect vanished. A 2024 Political Psychology replication and extension by Walker and colleagues reached the same split verdict: the illusion punctures, attitudes don't budge. The confidence collapse is apparently more robust than its downstream persuasive power.

Other researchers have gone looking for who is more susceptible and found measurable predictors. A 2024 study by Christian Gaviria and Javier Corredor in Memory & Cognition (255 college students) found that performance on a concurrent working-memory task fully mediated the size of the illusion — when cognitive load ate into working memory, the gap between predicted and actual understanding grew, while higher scores on the Cognitive Reflection Test and prior coursework in the relevant domain both shrank it. That lines up with the Dunning-Kruger literature conceptually but is a distinct mechanism: Dunning-Kruger is about miscalibrated skill assessment from people who lack the expertise to judge their own incompetence, while IOED is specifically about mistaking a fluent, compressed mental sketch of a causal chain for the chain itself. The conspiracy-theory literature has picked this up directly — Vitriol and Marsh, in the European Journal of Social Psychology (2018), showed that people with inflated confidence in their understanding of policy mechanisms were more likely to endorse general and election-specific conspiracy theories, especially political novices and people on the losing side of an election, suggesting the illusion isn't just an embarrassing party trick but a real on-ramp to motivated reasoning.

The newest and arguably highest-stakes extension is into explainable AI. Michael Chromik and colleagues at LMU Munich, in a 2021 CHI paper, "I Think I Get Your Point, AI! The Illusion of Explanatory Depth in Explainable AI," tested users of SHAP-based model explanations — the additive, feature-contribution charts meant to make machine-learning decisions legible to non-experts — and found the same signature: people felt they understood why a model made a decision until asked to predict the model's behavior on new, similar cases, at which point accuracy cratered and confidence fell back in line with it. Follow-on work on "machine explanations and human understanding" has generalized this into a warning for the entire interpretability industry: showing people a plausible-looking explanation of a black-box model creates a subjective sense of insight that outruns any actual gain in predictive understanding, meaning interpretability tools can increase trust without increasing competence. Medicine has adopted the same framing for informed consent — a 2023 letter in Eye by Rarichan, Bacchi, Gupta, and Chan argued that both patients and surgeons are vulnerable to the illusion during consent conversations for procedures like vitreoretinal surgery, and recommended the same diagnostic Rozenblit and Keil stumbled on by accident: ask the patient to explain the procedure back, rather than just rate their comfort with it, because the act of generating the explanation is what exposes the gap that a confidence rating alone will not.
