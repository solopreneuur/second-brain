# Better Place bet on one car

**The find:** Shai Agassi raised roughly $850 million to build battery-swap stations across Israel and Denmark, letting electric cars trade a dead battery for a charged one in minutes — but the entire network only worked with one car, a Renault built to Better Place's custom spec, because no other automaker would commit.
**The surprise:** The company sold fewer than 1,500 cars combined before its 2013 bankruptcy — not because electric cars or battery swapping were wrong, but because it built the whole network before it had more than one compatible vehicle.
**The idea:** [Right idea, wrong time](../../../concepts/right-idea-wrong-time.md) — except here the missing condition wasn't technology or cost, it was partners.

## In depth

In 2007, Shai Agassi quit SAP, where he had run the company's Product and Technology Group and sat on its executive board after being passed over for the CEO job, and founded Better Place to try to eliminate oil as a transportation fuel. His pitch, delivered at Davos and in a widely watched TED talk, treated the car as a mobile phone: drivers wouldn't own the battery, they would subscribe to miles the way they subscribe to minutes, swapping a depleted battery for a charged one at a robotic station in under five minutes. Investors bought in at scale: roughly $850 million in private capital, including a $200 million-plus Series A in 2007 led by VantagePoint Venture Partners and Israel Corporation (which became Better Place's largest shareholder, holding about a third of the company, under chairman Idan Ofer), a $350 million Series B in January 2010 that included $125 million from HSBC, plus money from Morgan Stanley, Lazard, General Electric, and Denmark's DONG Energy.

The entire network depended on one car. Renault, under CEO Carlos Ghosn, agreed to build the Fluence Z.E. to Better Place's swap-compatible spec — the only production vehicle in the world designed that way. No other automaker signed on. Better Place built roughly 21 operational swap stations in Israel at its peak (with dozens more planned) and 17 in Denmark, but sold or deployed fewer than 1,000 cars in Israel and around 400 in Denmark — nowhere near Agassi's early prediction of 100,000 Israeli cars on the road by 2010. By 2013, Ghosn had publicly soured, saying battery swapping was "no longer the main track" for EVs and that customers wanted flexibility rather than a single, exchangeable standard. With no second automaker and Renault's own enthusiasm gone, the company filed for bankruptcy in Israel on May 26, 2013; its assets were sold that November for $450,000. Agassi left the board shortly before the filing.

The idea itself wasn't wrong. More than a decade later, China's NIO operates over 4,000 swap stations and has logged over 130 million battery swaps, succeeding where Better Place failed because NIO builds the cars, owns the batteries, and runs the network itself — one company controlling the whole stack instead of a startup begging automakers to join.
