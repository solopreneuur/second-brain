# A meltdown that invented UX

**The find:** After the 1979 Three Mile Island partial meltdown, cognitive scientist Don Norman joined the investigation and found the control room's warning light for the stuck-open relief valve didn't show the valve's real position — it only lit when a close command had been sent, so operators believed a stuck-open valve was shut for hours.
**The surprise:** Norman concluded the operators hadn't failed at all; the interface had lied to them by reporting the command it received instead of the state of the world.
**The idea:** [Status illusion](../../../concepts/status-illusion.md) — a status indicator that shows the last command rather than the actual state isn't a minor UI quirk, it's the exact design flaw that can make a reactor, or a product, lie to the person using it.

## In depth

In March 1979, a stuck-open pilot-operated relief valve on Three Mile Island's Unit 2 reactor let coolant drain away for hours while the control room's indicator showed the valve closed — because the light was wired to show that a close signal had been sent, not that the valve had actually responded. Operators, trusting the panel, took actions based on a reactor state that didn't exist, and the core partially melted before anyone realized the mistake. The Kemeny Commission's investigation pulled in outside experts, among them cognitive scientist Don Norman, who had spent his career studying how people make errors using machines. Norman's read on the control room broke with the official instinct to blame the operators: the real failure was a design that reported its own commands back as if they were facts about the world. He began cataloguing similar "command versus state" failures across consumer products — stove dials that don't map to the burner they control, elevator buttons that light up whether or not they've registered a request — and built them into a broader argument, published as "The Psychology of Everyday Things" in 1988 (reissued as "The Design of Everyday Things"), that bad design, not bad users, causes most "human error." The book became the founding text of user-centered design and usability engineering as a discipline, and Norman later coined the term "user experience" itself while running Apple's User Experience Architect's Office in the 1990s. The nuclear industry also changed: post-TMI redesigns of control rooms focused specifically on making indicators show true system state, and the "don't confuse a command with its result" principle is now a standard heuristic taught in interface design courses, decades removed from the reactor it came from.
