# The economist who measured light

**The find:** Yale economist William Nordhaus built ancient-style oil lamps in his own home, measured their light output with a photometer, and tracked how many hours of labor it took to buy 1,000 lumen-hours of light across history.
**The surprise:** Standard price indices said light got cheaper by a modest multiple since 1800; Nordhaus's time-price measure showed it actually got roughly 350,000 times cheaper, because currency-based prices miss almost all real quality improvement.
**The idea:** Measuring value in the wrong unit (money, not time or effort saved) can hide almost all of the real progress a product has made — a caution for any business that reports its value only in price.

## In depth

In 1994 (published 1996 in the NBER volume *The Economics of New Goods*), Yale economist William Nordhaus wrote "Do Real-Output and Real-Wage Measures Capture Reality? The History of Lighting Suggests Not." To build a true price series for light going back to 1750 BC, he didn't just compile historical data — he ran his own experiments. Using a Minolta TL-1 illuminance meter, he burned 21 pounds of firewood in his home fireplace and measured 1.7 lumen-hours per pound. He bought a Roman terra-cotta oil lamp, filled it with cold-pressed sesame oil, and burned it for 17 hours to get its lumen output, standing in for the Babylonian sesame-oil lamps of 1750 BC. He combined these with Benjamin Silliman Jr.'s rediscovered 1855 Yale experiments on candles, whale oil, camphene and kerosene, plus data on Edison's 1883 carbon-filament bulb and modern compact fluorescents.

The results: a Babylonian laborer earning one shekel a month, with sesame oil costing about half a shekel for five liters, had to work roughly 41–58 hours to produce 1,000 lumen-hours of light. By 1800, a tallow candle brought that to about 5.4 hours. By 1900, electric filament lamps cut it to roughly 0.22 hours (13 minutes). By 1992, a compact fluorescent bulb required just 0.00012 hours — about four seconds of work. That's roughly a 350,000-fold drop in time-price from ancient Babylon to the present by Nordhaus's own estimate, far beyond what conventional price indices captured; by his calculation, official indices overstated lighting's price rise by a factor of 900 to 1,600 since 1800, because they track input (fuel) prices, not output (lumens) — missing both quality improvements and the efficiency leaps of new goods like electric filaments replacing kerosene.

This lighting study is a separate strand of Nordhaus's career from his 2018 Nobel (shared with Paul Romer, for integrating climate change into economic models). Nordhaus died October 30, 2026, age 85, after more than 50 years at Yale — prompting NPR Planet Money's November 4, 2026 retrospective.
