# Ask for exactly 17 cents

**The find:** Researchers had a confederate panhandle on a Santa Cruz wharf. Asking "can you spare any change?" got 44% compliance; asking "can you spare 17 cents?" (or 37 cents) pushed compliance to about 75%.
**The surprise:** A smaller, weirder-sounding ask beat the normal, rounder one — specificity outperformed size.
**The idea:** The [Pique technique](../../../concepts/pique-technique.md) — an unusual, oddly specific request breaks the "mindless refusal" script people run on autopilot, long enough to earn real attention.

9 · Unfamiliar concept · Psychology · [Santos, Leve & Pratkanis, 1994](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1559-1816.1994.tb00610.x)

## In depth

Most refusals to a panhandler aren't decisions — they're reflexes. "Spare any change?" has been heard so many times that people answer it the way they answer a spam call: before they've really processed the words. In 1994, social psychologists Michael Santos, Carl Leve and Anthony Pratkanis tested whether breaking that script with an oddly specific number would force real processing instead of autopilot refusal. A researcher posing as a panhandler on a Santa Cruz pier asked passersby for either a normal, round amount ("any spare change," or "a quarter") or a strange, unrounded one ("17 cents," or "37 cents"). The round-number requests got roughly 44% compliance, in line with typical panhandling studies. The pique requests — the odd, specific ones — pushed compliance up to around 75%, and people who complied with the odd request often gave more than was asked for.

The researchers named this the "pique technique," from "piquing" curiosity: an unusual request disrupts the automatic script just long enough that the person actually engages with the question rather than reflexively saying no. A normal request pattern-matches instantly to "panhandler, decline." An odd one — why 17 cents, specifically? — doesn't pattern-match to anything, so the brain has to actually process it, and that moment of genuine attention is enough to shift the outcome. A 2017 meta-analysis by Sumi Lee and Thomas Feeley found the pique technique's effect size (r = .27) outperforms the much more commonly cited foot-in-the-door technique (r = .11), despite pique being far less known outside academic psychology.

The mechanism travels well beyond panhandling. It shows up in why an odd price ending ($13.47 instead of a round $15 off) or a strangely specific subject line can outperform the expected, rounder version — not because the number itself matters, but because its oddness forces a beat of real attention a normal-sounding ask never gets.
