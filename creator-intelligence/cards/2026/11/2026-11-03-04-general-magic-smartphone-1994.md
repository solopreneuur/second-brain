# The smartphone that died in 1994

**The find:** General Magic, a 1990 Apple spinoff staffed by original Macintosh engineers, built and shipped a pocket touchscreen communicator with email, apps and a mail client in 1994 — roughly a decade before cellular data existed to support it. It sold about 3,000 units and folded by 2002.
**The surprise:** The "failed" company's engineers seeded the next 25 years of tech directly — alumni include the people who later built the iPod, iPhone, Nest, Android and eBay.
**The idea:** [Right idea, wrong time](../../../concepts/right-idea-wrong-time.md) — a product can be correct about the future and still die, because the infrastructure around it hasn't caught up yet.

## In depth

General Magic was founded in May 1990 as a spinoff from Apple, built around a project Marc Porat had run internally called "Paradigm." Porat convinced Apple CEO John Sculley that the next computing wave needed a joint venture spanning computing, telecom and consumer electronics rather than Apple alone. Apple took a minority stake, and Sculley joined the board. Co-founders Andy Hertzfeld and Bill Atkinson had been core Macintosh architects — Atkinson wrote QuickDraw, MacPaint and HyperCard; Hertzfeld helped build the original Mac's system software. Motorola, Sony, AT&T, Philips and Matsushita were partners and investors.

The company built an operating system, Magic Cap, using a "rooms" metaphor — desk, hallway, notebook — instead of a desktop, plus a mobile-agent programming language called Telescript that let code migrate between devices and network servers. Two devices shipped in 1994: the Sony Magic Link and the Motorola Envoy. Magic Link ran a 16MHz processor, had a 480x320 touchscreen, 1MB of RAM, a 2400-bps modem, ran on six AAA batteries, and included email, an address book, a notebook, a spreadsheet and an AOL mail client. The Envoy added wireless data over the ARDIS network, with service fees reportedly running over $400 a month. Neither device had true cellular data — the networks of the era couldn't support it.

Commercially, the devices flopped: roughly 3,000 Magic Link units sold, mostly to employees' friends and family, within about six months, at a price around $800–$1,000. General Magic still went public in February 1995, raising $96 million on no real product revenue, then declined through the late 1990s before shutting down operations in September 2002.

Alumni fanned out across the industry: Tony Fadell (iPod, iPhone, Nest), Andy Rubin (Android), Pierre Omidyar (wrote eBay's original code while employed there), Megan Smith (later US CTO), and Kevin Lynch (Adobe CTO, later Apple Watch). A 2018 documentary, directed by Sarah Kerruish and Matt Maude, covers the company's rise and fall.
