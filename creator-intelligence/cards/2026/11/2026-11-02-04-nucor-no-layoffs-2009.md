# Nucor cut pay, not people

**The find:** Nucor posted a $293.6 million net loss in 2009 but laid off none of its roughly 20,000 workers, instead cutting hours and the production bonuses that normally make up about two-thirds of a steelworker's take-home pay.
**The surprise:** Workers absorbed the recession through their own bonus checks, a shock absorber built directly into the pay structure, long criticized by Wall Street as inefficient.
**The idea:** [Painsharing](../../../concepts/painsharing.md): make compensation variable in good times, and the organisation has built-in slack to protect jobs in bad times.

3 · Wrong decision that worked · Founders · [Bloomberg, "Pain, But No Layoffs, at Nucor"](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2009-03-25/pain-but-no-layoffs-at-nucor)

## In depth

Nucor pays steelworkers a base wage that's low relative to the industry, but layers on production bonuses tied to the output and quality of each worker's team — in good years those bonuses can make up roughly two-thirds of a worker's total take-home pay. The system, built by CEO Ken Iverson starting in the 1960s–70s, ties pay directly to output rather than seniority, runs on a famously flat management structure (Iverson kept corporate headquarters staff under 25 people for a multibillion-dollar company), and includes profit sharing on top of the bonus.

The 2008–2009 financial crisis hit Nucor hard: after a record 2008, the company posted a full-year 2009 net loss of $293.6 million, or $0.94 per diluted share, as steel demand collapsed. Nucor did not lay off any of its roughly 20,000 employees for lack of work. Instead it cut hours, and because bonuses are tied to production, bonuses evaporated — some reporting put total worker pay reductions at around 40% for the year. Executives and salaried staff absorbed cuts too, following the same logic Nucor used in a worse crisis decades earlier: during the 1982 steel collapse, when the industry shed over 100,000 jobs and companies like Bethlehem Steel and Republic Steel disappeared, Nucor cut department-head pay up to 40%, general managers' and officers' pay 50–60%, and Iverson's own salary from about $450,000 to about $110,000 — while still not laying anyone off.

Wall Street and industry analysts have periodically criticized Nucor's model as inefficient compared to rivals who simply shed headcount in downturns, arguing that carrying full employment through a trough depresses short-term margins. Nucor's counter, borne out over decades, is that the policy buys worker loyalty and flexibility that lowers its cost per ton over the full cycle — it has never shut a plant or laid off a worker for lack of work in more than 50 years, spanning the 1980s steel crisis, 2001, 2008–09, and the 2015–16 steel slump.
