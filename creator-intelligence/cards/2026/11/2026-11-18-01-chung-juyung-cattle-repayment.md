# Hyundai's founder repaid a stolen cow

**The find:** As a teenager, Hyundai founder Chung Ju-yung stole one of his father's cows and sold it for 70 won to fund his escape to Seoul. In June 1998, at age 82, he personally led a convoy of trucks across the DMZ into North Korea carrying 1,001 head of cattle.
**The surprise:** The number wasn't round or symbolic in the usual sense — it was exactly one more than the thousand he decided he owed, a literal interest payment on a seven-decade-old debt to his own father.
**The idea:** Guilt compounds like a debt: a single small theft can generate a lifetime obligation that eventually gets repaid at a scale wildly disproportionate to the original act ([Symbolic restitution](../../../concepts/symbolic-restitution.md)).

## In depth

Chung Ju-yung was born in 1915 to a poor farming family in what is now North Korea. As a teenager he tried to run away from the farm at least twice before his cow heist succeeded: his father caught him after he sold rice for travel money, and again after a second escape attempt. On the third try, around age 18, he took one of the family's cows to a market, sold it for 70 won, and used the money to reach Seoul, where he began the string of odd jobs — including running a rice delivery shop — that eventually grew into the Hyundai conglomerate: construction, shipbuilding, and the car company that still carries the name today.

By his own account, he never stopped feeling guilty about the cow. In June 1998, after South Korea's new "Sunshine Policy" of engagement with the North created an opening, Chung — then chairman of Hyundai and 82 years old — organized a cattle drive across the heavily militarized Panmunjom border crossing. Fifty trucks carried 1,001 cattle into North Korea: 500 for the North's livestock-starved farms, 500 more pledged for later delivery, and the extra head explicitly framed as interest on the one he'd taken as a boy. Reporters covering the event noted Chung himself walked across the border alongside the first truck. The gesture was also calculated business diplomacy — it helped pave the way for Hyundai's subsequent Mount Kumgang tourism venture, one of the first major inter-Korean commercial projects. Chung died in 2001; his cattle drive is still cited in Korean business histories as the clearest public expression of the frugality-and-debt ethic that shaped Hyundai's early culture.
