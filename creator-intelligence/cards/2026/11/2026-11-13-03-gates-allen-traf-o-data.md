# Bill Gates's failed traffic-counter company

**The find:** Before Microsoft, teenage Bill Gates and Paul Allen built a machine that read traffic-counter paper tape and sold the reports to cities. It lost money and died once Washington State started offering the same service free.
**The surprise:** The commercial failure didn't matter — the only thing that carried forward was a software trick (simulating a new chip on a mainframe before owning the hardware), and that exact trick is how they wrote Microsoft's first product without ever touching an Altair computer.
**The idea:** A failed venture can hand you nothing but one specific, transferable skill — and that skill alone can be the whole reason the next thing works ([Forced detour](../../../concepts/forced-detour.md)).

## In depth

In 1972, Bill Gates and Paul Allen, teenage students at Lakeside School in Seattle, teamed up with Paul Gilbert, a University of Washington electrical engineering undergraduate, to found Traf-O-Data on June 13, 1972. The idea came from a real annoyance: Gates had already been paid to process traffic data by hand. Roadway counters recorded vehicle axles on pressure-sensitive rubber road tubes, punching the results every fifteen minutes onto 16-channel paper tape. Someone then had to read that tape manually and copy the numbers onto cards — Gates had farmed the job out to younger Lakeside students, paying them fifty cents a tape. The trio decided to automate it by building a machine around the brand-new Intel 8008 microprocessor, with Gilbert wire-wrapping the actual hardware. While the physical machine was still being built, Allen adapted the PDP-10 Macro Assembler and DDT debugger at a local university terminal to write a software simulator of the 8008, letting Gates write and debug the processing code before any hardware existed.

The finished box read the tape and printed traffic-volume reports for sale to cities and towns, but the business never took off — only a handful of municipal clients ever paid for it, and by the mid-1970s the State of Washington began offering the same traffic-counting service to municipalities for free, killing the market. Across its life, Traf-O-Data brought in roughly $6,631 in gross receipts against costs, netting a cumulative loss of about $3,494; the partnership was formally wound down years later, in 1982, with each partner receiving a final payout of a few hundred dollars.

The payoff wasn't money — it was the simulator technique. In late 1974, after seeing the Altair 8800 on the cover of Popular Electronics, Gates and Allen told MITS they had a BASIC interpreter ready, despite having neither an Altair nor working code. Allen then adapted his old 8008 emulator into one for the Altair's Intel 8080 chip, running it on Harvard's PDP-10, and the two wrote and debugged Altair BASIC entirely on that simulator. It only touched real Altair hardware for the first time at the demo in Albuquerque in 1975 — and it worked, becoming Microsoft's founding product.
