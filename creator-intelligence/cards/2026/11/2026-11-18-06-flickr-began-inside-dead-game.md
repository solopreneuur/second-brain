# Flickr began inside a dead game

**The find:** Stewart Butterfield, Caterina Fake and Jason Classon founded Ludicorp in 2002 to build "Game Neverending," a web-based multiplayer game with no win condition. When the game failed to gain traction, they extracted its real-time photo-sharing and chat feature, renamed it Flickr, and launched it in February 2004.
**The surprise:** The founders spent two years building an entire game to discover the throwaway side-feature inside it was the actual product.
**The idea:** When the main thing fails, look at what people kept using anyway — it's often more valuable than what you set out to build ([Accidental product](../../../concepts/accidental-product.md)).

## In depth

Ludicorp was founded in Vancouver in 2002 by Stewart Butterfield, Caterina Fake and Jason Classon with the goal of building "Game Neverending," a browser-based massively multiplayer game explicitly designed around social play rather than winning — closer to a persistent social space than a competitive game. To support players chatting and sharing images with each other inside the game, the team built lightweight real-time photo upload and messaging tools. As development dragged on and the game itself struggled to find an audience, the team noticed those support tools were the part generating genuine enthusiasm.

In February 2004 they stripped the photo-sharing and chat functionality out, rebuilt it as a standalone product, and launched it as Flickr — among the first services to combine fast photo uploading, tagging, and social sharing in one place, years before Instagram or any mobile-first photo app existed. Flickr grew quickly enough that Yahoo acquired Ludicorp and Flickr in March 2005, reportedly for between $25 million and $35 million, folding the photo service into Yahoo's broader portfolio (where it later struggled under a string of ownership changes before eventually landing with SmugMug in 2018).

The pattern repeated almost exactly for Butterfield a decade later: his next company, Tiny Speck, spent three years and tens of millions of dollars building "Glitch," a whimsical browser MMO that shut down in December 2012 for lack of audience. The internal chat tool Tiny Speck had built to coordinate Glitch's own development team became Slack, launched in 2013 and eventually valued north of $27 billion when Salesforce acquired it in 2021. Butterfield has described both pivots the same way: when the thing you're deliberately building doesn't work, look hard at the tool you built by accident to keep your own team running — that's sometimes the real company.
