# Dorsey ran Twitter on NFL rules

**The find:** Jack Dorsey installed Bill Walsh's 1979 San Francisco 49ers "Standard of Performance" — literal rules like pushing in your chair after a meeting — as the operating model at both Square and Twitter, after Keith Rabois gave him Walsh's book when Rabois joined Square.
**The surprise:** Two major tech companies' culture codes were lifted almost verbatim from a decades-old NFL locker-room turnaround manual.
**The idea:** [Cross-domain transfer](../../../concepts/cross-domain-transfer.md): a practice from an unrelated field carries its principle wholesale into a new one.

7 · Far-field · Founders · [Sequoia Capital](https://articles.sequoiacap.com/2019-10-09-jack-dorsey)

## In depth

Bill Walsh took over the San Francisco 49ers in 1979 as head coach and general manager. The team had gone 2-14 the year before and had won just 31 of its previous 86 games — one of the worst franchises in the NFL. Walsh didn't start by recruiting star talent or redesigning the playbook. He wrote down a list called the "Standard of Performance": specific, literal behaviors every person in the organization had to follow, regardless of role or title. The list included things like tucking in your shirt, pulling your socks all the way up, showing up on time, no strutting or posturing after a play, no profanity, no fighting, and treating fans with professional respect. Walsh's reasoning, laid out later in his book *The Score Takes Care of Itself*, was that "champions behave like champions before they're champions" — you install the standard of performance first, and winning follows as a byproduct, not a precondition. Three years after he was hired, the 49ers won Super Bowl XVI; Walsh went on to win three Super Bowls in ten years with a 102-63-1 record.

Keith Rabois gave Jack Dorsey Walsh's book when Rabois joined Square as its 23rd employee and COO. Dorsey cited it publicly at least twice: at Y Combinator's Startup School in October 2013, where he discussed the book among those that shaped how he thought about building teams, and in Sequoia's "Seven Questions with Jack Dorsey" piece from October 2019. In that interview Dorsey said a detail "as simple as pushing in your chair when you leave a meeting room... shows you're respecting the people who come in after you and the shared space where we all create," adding, "If you're paying attention to a detail like that, it helps you pay attention to details in the service or software as well. And with that daily practice, as we get better and better at those little things, they ultimately become the big things." He installed this logic at both Square and Twitter: setting explicit, literal behavioral rules for how people act in meetings and physical space, on the theory that small enforced standards compound into the organizational discipline that produces good product and business outcomes. The underlying mechanism he borrowed wholesale from football into tech: process and behavioral standards are leading indicators you can control directly; the scoreboard — wins, revenue — is a lagging indicator you can't.
