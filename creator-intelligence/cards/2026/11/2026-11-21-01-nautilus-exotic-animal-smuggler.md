# The Smuggler Who Built Nautilus

**The find:** Arthur Jones spent 20 years tinkering with resistance machines as a side hobby while running an exotic-animal import business, flying captured elephants, cheetahs and crocodiles out of Africa and South America in old B-25 bombers.
**The surprise:** The gym machine in every commercial gym exists because his animal business got seized by the Rhodesian government in 1968, and the forced downtime let him finally finish the prototype.
**The idea:** A [forced detour](../../../concepts/forced-detour.md) — losing the thing you were actually building your life around can be what finishes the thing that makes you famous.

## In depth

Before Nautilus, Arthur Jones ran "The Reptile Jungle," an exotic-animal roadside zoo and import-export operation in Slidell, Louisiana, that supplied wild animals to zoos and circuses. He documented the work in his own syndicated TV series, "Wild Cargo" (debuted February 1961), filming expeditions across the Congo and other parts of Africa, South America, Mexico and Australia to capture elephants, cheetahs, leopards, crocodiles and primates, often flying the animals himself in old B-25 bombers. A 1975 Sports Illustrated profile noted Jones had been "bitten by rattlesnakes, lions, men and other dangerous critters" and had come near death repeatedly over his career. In the mid-1960s he relocated to Rhodesia (now Zimbabwe) to continue the business; around 1968, the Rhodesian government seized his operation's assets — reportedly about $2 million in airplanes, a helicopter and film equipment — amid the country's political turmoil, forcing him back to the United States essentially broke.

That forced exit from the animal trade gave Jones the downtime to finish two decades of machine tinkering. He unveiled his prototype, nicknamed "the Blue Monster," at the 1970 Mr. America contest in Culver City, California; it used chains, cams and sprockets to vary resistance through a lift's full range of motion. The first production Nautilus machine was delivered to a customer on November 30, 1970. Gym owners largely dismissed Jones as a crank, but the Kansas City Chiefs, coached by Hank Stram, bought a set of machines that year — a high-profile NFL endorsement that gave the invention credibility it couldn't get from bodybuilders alone.

Nautilus Sports/Medical Industries grew into the world's largest exercise-equipment maker, with roughly 4,700 licensed Nautilus Fitness Centers by 1984; contemporary estimates of annual sales ranged from about $300 million (Time, 1984) to around $400 million by the mid-1980s. Jones sold the company in July 1986 for $23 million and used the proceeds to fund MedX, a rehabilitation-equipment venture, before his death in 2007 at age 80.
