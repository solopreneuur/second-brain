# America's top divorce lawyer calls prenups romantic

**The find:** James Sexton, a New York divorce attorney billed as America's top-rated, tells The Diary of a CEO that discussing a prenup with a partner is "quite romantic," because it means facing the odds of divorce together instead of denying them.
**The surprise:** The person whose career runs on broken marriages argues the most loving thing a couple can do is talk about the marriage ending.
**The idea:** [Radical honesty](../../../concepts/radical-honesty.md): naming the hard outcome out loud, together, builds more trust than pretending it can't happen.

## In depth

James Sexton is a New York divorce attorney who has practiced family law since 2001, holds a JD from Fordham Law and a master's focused on persuasive speech, and is frequently billed in media appearances as America's top-rated or top-1% divorce lawyer. He's written two books built on patterns he says he's seen across two decades of divorce cases: "If You're in My Office, It's Already Too Late: A Divorce Lawyer's Guide to Staying Together" (2019), and "How to Stay in Love." His pitch is consistent across both: he's watched marriages fail at close range, and uses that vantage point to give blunt advice on staying together rather than just litigating the aftermath.

On Steven Bartlett's Diary of a CEO, Sexton argues a prenup is not a betrayal of romantic faith but an act of trust. He frames it by asking who you trust more to decide the financial terms of your marriage: a state legislature's default divorce statutes, written by people who don't know you, or your own partner, negotiating directly with you while you both still like each other. In his framing, avoiding a prenup because it seems unromantic actually means handing the decision to the government instead of making it together. He adds that no US wedding expo would sell him a vendor table to discuss prenups, even for cash, because "it shatters the illusion."

In the same episode he discusses divorce statistics, citing a roughly 56% divorce rate, and describes "slippage" as the real mechanism behind most of the failures he's seen: not one dramatic betrayal, but an accumulation of small, individually meaningless disconnections — a partner slowly sliding down a person's list of priorities — that compound until the relationship has quietly ended long before anyone files paperwork. He's made versions of this same argument across other podcast and TV appearances tied to his two books.

## Deeper · 2026-11-02

The adoption numbers back up Sexton's "everyone has a prenup" pitch in ways that go beyond his anecdote about couples stopping him on the street. According to a LegalShield survey covered by Newsweek, roughly 39% of millennials now have a prenup versus just 4% of baby boomers — a tenfold jump — and the same survey found couples carrying student debt were six times more likely to sign one, with 54% saying they'd get a prenup specifically to protect a partner from their debt. A 2023 Axios-Harris Poll found 47% of engaged or married millennials and 41% of Gen Z adults had already signed one, and reported that overall public support for prenups rose from 42% to 50% in a single year. The American Academy of Matrimonial Lawyers has separately reported a 62% jump in client requests for prenups, with more than half of surveyed attorneys naming millennials as the group driving it. One dataset even found more than 52% of women now initiate the prenup conversation, flipping the old assumption that it's the wealthier partner — usually coded male — forcing the issue. Sexton is describing a real demographic shift, not just a sales pitch he invented for a podcast.

The claim that talking about a prenup is itself relationship-healthy is far less settled than Sexton makes it sound. A brief report titled "For Love or Money: Prenuptial Agreements and Marital Quality," published in the journal Family Relations in 2026 by researcher Jeffrey Dew, is among the first empirical studies to actually test this. It found prenups were not associated with worse marital satisfaction for couples who already reported high satisfaction with their partner's commitment or communication — but for everyone else, having a prenup correlated with lower perceived marital stability. In other words, a prenup doesn't appear to cause harm in an already-strong relationship, but it's not shown to create the trust Sexton describes either; it mostly tracks whatever trust already existed. That cuts against the "radical honesty builds trust" framing and toward a more mundane reading: good communicators do fine with prenups, and people who can't communicate well don't suddenly fix that by signing one. A separate line of research on Orthodox Jewish couples who sign a religious prenuptial agreement (a "beit din" document meant to prevent one spouse from refusing a future religious divorce) found higher marital satisfaction among signers, published in the Journal of Divorce & Remarriage — but that is a very different instrument, signed for religious-obligation reasons rather than asset division, and isn't a clean analog to Sexton's commercial prenups. Academic critiques elsewhere go further than skepticism: researchers studying the psychology of prenups have argued the agreements are typically sought by the financially stronger partner to protect assets, and that framing the request as "practical" or "romantic" can mask preexisting power imbalances rather than resolve them.

Sexton's other headline number — "almost 60% of marriages end in divorce" — is also contested terrain among demographers, and not in his favor. University of Michigan economist Justin Wolfers and University of Michigan (later Pennsylvania) economist Betsey Stevenson have published data showing the 50%-plus figure is a holdover from the divorce spike of the 1970s-80s and has been wrong for years: divorce rates have fallen roughly 24% over the last three decades, and if trends hold, about two-thirds of marriages that began in the 2000s will not end in divorce. Stevenson testified to the Joint Economic Committee of Congress on this point in 2020, attributing the earlier spike to women entering the workforce and gaining more legal and economic independence, with rates stabilizing as society adjusted. Sexton isn't alone in using the higher figure — it's the version most people still believe — but it means his two central numbers (divorce prevalence, and prenups as rational insurance against it) are more debatable than he presents them, since the actual odds for a given couple marrying today are meaningfully better than "almost 60%," especially for college-educated, later-marrying couples, which skews lower still.

The psychological mechanism Sexton is describing — that naming a feared outcome together defuses it rather than inviting it — has a real research base, just not one he cites. It closely mirrors the "premortem" technique formalized by decision psychologist Gary Klein in a widely cited Harvard Business Review piece: by assuming failure as a settled fact and working backward from it, people become willing to voice risks they'd otherwise suppress, because the past-tense framing removes the social cost of pessimism. Klein found premortems surface roughly a third more risks than standard planning reviews. Tim Ferriss's "fear-setting" exercise, which he traces to the Stoic practice of premeditatio malorum ("premeditation of evils") described by Seneca, works on the same logic in a personal-anxiety context: writing out a worst-case scenario in concrete detail tends to shrink the fear rather than feed it, because vague dread is scarier than a defined, specific outcome. That logic is also the backbone of exposure-based cognitive behavioral therapy, where structured, repeated confrontation with a feared scenario reduces avoidance and anxiety over time. None of this research is about marriage specifically, but it's the same underlying claim Sexton is making about prenup conversations: that naming the worst case out loud with another person, instead of avoiding it, is what actually lowers anxiety about it. Sexton isn't the first to market prenups this way, either — New York matrimonial attorney Arlene Dubin published "Prenups for Lovers: A Romantic Guide to Prenuptial Agreements" in 2001, arguing the negotiation process itself stimulates the communication and compromise that make marriages stronger, two decades before Sexton made the same pitch to Steven Bartlett.
