# America's top divorce lawyer calls prenups romantic

**The find:** James Sexton, a New York divorce attorney billed as America's top-rated, tells The Diary of a CEO that discussing a prenup with a partner is "quite romantic," because it means facing the odds of divorce together instead of denying them.
**The surprise:** The person whose career runs on broken marriages argues the most loving thing a couple can do is talk about the marriage ending.
**The idea:** [Radical honesty](../../../concepts/radical-honesty.md): naming the hard outcome out loud, together, builds more trust than pretending it can't happen.

## In depth

James Sexton is a New York divorce attorney who has practiced family law since 2001, holds a JD from Fordham Law and a master's focused on persuasive speech, and is frequently billed in media appearances as America's top-rated or top-1% divorce lawyer. He's written two books built on patterns he says he's seen across two decades of divorce cases: "If You're in My Office, It's Already Too Late: A Divorce Lawyer's Guide to Staying Together" (2019), and "How to Stay in Love." His pitch is consistent across both: he's watched marriages fail at close range, and uses that vantage point to give blunt advice on staying together rather than just litigating the aftermath.

On Steven Bartlett's Diary of a CEO, Sexton argues a prenup is not a betrayal of romantic faith but an act of trust. He frames it by asking who you trust more to decide the financial terms of your marriage: a state legislature's default divorce statutes, written by people who don't know you, or your own partner, negotiating directly with you while you both still like each other. In his framing, avoiding a prenup because it seems unromantic actually means handing the decision to the government instead of making it together. He adds that no US wedding expo would sell him a vendor table to discuss prenups, even for cash, because "it shatters the illusion."

In the same episode he discusses divorce statistics, citing a roughly 56% divorce rate, and describes "slippage" as the real mechanism behind most of the failures he's seen: not one dramatic betrayal, but an accumulation of small, individually meaningless disconnections — a partner slowly sliding down a person's list of priorities — that compound until the relationship has quietly ended long before anyone files paperwork. He's made versions of this same argument across other podcast and TV appearances tied to his two books.
