# Valve's bossless crisis cost $10,000

**The find:** Valve has run without managers or job titles since 1996. After George Floyd's murder in 2020, 16 employees told investigators the flat structure meant months of deadlock over whether to issue a public statement — there was no one with the authority to force a decision. It ended with every employee getting $10,000 to donate instead.
**The surprise:** The company's anti-hierarchy structure, built for speed and autonomy, turned out to be paralyzing in the one moment that needed a decision.
**The idea:** [Decentralised control](../../../concepts/decentralised-control.md): removing a decision-maker removes decisions, not just hierarchy.

## In depth

In January 2023, UK outlet People Make Games — founded in 2018 by former Eurogamer journalists Chris Bratt and Anni Sayers, joined later by former Rock Paper Shotgun writer Quintin Smith — published a roughly hour-long video investigation into Valve's workplace culture, built on interviews with 16 current and former employees speaking anonymously. The channel is viewer-funded through Patreon and has a track record of investigative pieces on crunch, outsourcing, and labor conditions across the games industry.

Valve has operated without managers, job titles, or formal reporting lines since 1996, as laid out in its "Valve Handbook for New Employees": nobody "reports to" anybody, including the founders, and desks sit on wheels so employees can physically roll themselves toward whatever project currently needs them. Compensation is set by peer "stack ranking" rather than a boss's evaluation. People Make Games' sources described the lived reality as closer to "Lord of the Flies": without official authority, power accrues informally to whoever has tenure, reputation, or social capital, and getting anything approved means knowing who to informally lobby rather than following a clear chain of command. Multiple sources said this produced a workforce that hires in its own image — overwhelmingly white and male, with one person recalling never more than one female programmer on staff at a time — and a graveyard of canceled projects because no one had the authority to keep scattered, self-selected teams aligned.

The George Floyd episode was the starkest case: employees wanted a public statement, senior staff quietly opposed one, and with no boss able to force a resolution, the disagreement just sat unresolved for months. It ended not with a statement but a payout — Valve gave every employee $10,000 to donate to a charity of their choosing, sidestepping the need for the company itself to take a position.
