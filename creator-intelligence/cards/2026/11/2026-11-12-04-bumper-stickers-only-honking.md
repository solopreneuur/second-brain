# Political bumper stickers just make people honk

**The find:** In a driving-simulation study, researchers had a car cut off participants while displaying no sticker, a "Proud Democrat" sticker, a "Proud Republican" sticker, or a neutral "I love my dog" sticker. People reported being significantly more likely to honk at a car with the opposing party's sticker. A same-party sticker produced no measurable boost in warm feelings at all.
**The surprise:** A political identity badge on a car doesn't buy goodwill from your own side, it only buys hostility from the other side.
**The idea:** Visible identity signals work as a one-way weapon against out-groups more reliably than they work as a bonding device for in-groups ([Identity signalling](../../../concepts/identity-signalling.md)).

## In depth

University of Cincinnati researchers Rachel Torres and Ben Farrer designed a driving simulation to isolate what a visible political identity marker actually does to strangers on the road. Participants watched a car cut them off in traffic, with the only variable being what was stuck to its bumper: nothing, a "Proud Democrat" sticker, a "Proud Republican" sticker, or an apolitical control sticker reading "I love my dog." After each scenario, participants reported how likely they'd be to honk, and how warmly or coldly they felt toward the driver.

The asymmetry was the headline result. When the sticker signaled the opposing political party, participants reported a significantly higher likelihood of honking than in the no-sticker or same-party conditions. But when the sticker signaled their own party, there was no corresponding increase in warmth, patience, or charitable interpretation of the driver's mistake. Sharing a political identity with a stranger who just cut you off did nothing to soften the reaction; disagreeing with that stranger's identity made the reaction measurably worse.

This cuts against a common marketing and political-organizing assumption: that visible identity signals (a flag, a slogan, a logo) build affinity and solidarity among people who already share the identity. The study suggests the bonding half of that equation is weak or absent in real interactions, while the hostility half toward out-groups is reliable and easy to trigger. A brand or campaign that leans on identity signalling to rally its own side may be getting less of a "we" boost than it assumes, while reliably generating a "them" reaction from everyone else — a cost that's easy to underweight because it shows up in other people's behavior, not the campaign's own metrics.

The paper was published in October 2025 and had not yet circulated widely in marketing or psychology content as of this writing.
