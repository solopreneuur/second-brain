# Watermelons needed a laser label

**The find:** In an 8-week field experiment across 60 watermelon markets in Shijiazhuang, China, sellers given an expensive laser-cut quality label raised their melon quality and profits; sellers given a cheap sticker label did nothing differently.
**The surprise:** Once the laser labels were withdrawn the next season, quality collapsed straight back to baseline — the label itself, not the information on it, was doing the work.
**The idea:** [Quality signalling](../../../concepts/quality-signalling.md) only works if faking the signal is expensive — a cheap label is worse than no label, because buyers learn to ignore it.

## In depth

Watermelons are a classic "lemons" problem: a buyer can't tell a good melon from a bad one until they've cut it open at home, long after paying. Economists studying 60 real watermelon markets in Shijiazhuang, China, used this to test an old idea from information economics in the most literal setting possible. Half the sellers in the study were given a cheap, easily-copied paper sticker to mark their melons as high quality. The other half were given an expensive, hard-to-fake laser-etched label — the kind that takes real equipment and a verified harvest to produce.

Over the eight-week field experiment, the sellers with cheap stickers changed nothing about their actual melons: quality and pricing stayed where they'd started, because any seller — good or bad — could print the same sticker, so the label carried no real information and buyers priced it accordingly. The sellers given the costly laser label behaved completely differently: they genuinely raised the quality of the melons they brought to market and captured higher profits for doing so, because the label was credible precisely because a seller selling bad melons couldn't be bothered, or couldn't afford, to fake it.

The sharpest part of the result came the following season, when researchers withdrew the laser labels from the sellers who'd had them. Quality didn't drift down gradually — it collapsed straight back to the original baseline almost immediately, as if the intervening season of real quality improvement had never happened. The label wasn't informing buyers about quality that already existed; it was the entire incentive mechanism forcing quality to exist in the first place. Remove the costly signal and the underlying behavior it was propping up disappears with it.

The implication for any quality claim — "artisan," "organic," "handmade," a warranty seal — is that cost of acquisition, not the words on the label, is what makes a signal trustworthy. A badge anyone can slap on for free isn't a quality signal; it's decoration, and buyers calibrate to that within a season.
