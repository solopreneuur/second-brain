# Detection dogs smelled what handlers believed

**The find:** In a 2011 UC Davis study, 18 certified police detection-dog teams searched four rooms for drug or explosive scent. Handlers were told up to three spots per room might hold scent; in reality no room held any real scent anywhere, only a decoy marker meant to plant a false belief in the handler and a separate food or toy decoy meant to tempt the dog's own nose.
**The surprise:** Across 144 searches the teams produced 225 false alerts, clustering roughly twice as often at the spot the handler had been told to believe held scent as at the spot actually tempting the dog — the handler's belief steered the dog, not the other way around.
**The idea:** A real-world Clever Hans effect ([Expectancy effect](../../../concepts/expectancy-effect.md)): even an instrument built to be objective can be unconsciously steered by the belief of whoever is holding it.

## In depth

In 2011, three University of California, Davis researchers — Lisa Lit, a postdoctoral researcher in the Department of Neurology and a former detection-dog handler herself, Julie B. Schweitzer of the UC Davis MIND Institute, and Anita M. Oberbauer, chair of the campus's Department of Animal Science — published "Handler Beliefs Affect Scent Detection Dog Outcomes" in the journal Animal Cognition. They recruited 18 experienced, certified law-enforcement handler-dog teams: 13 worked narcotics detection, three worked explosives, and two did both. The dogs were mostly Labrador retrievers, Belgian Malinois, German shepherds and Dutch shepherds, with two to seven years of working experience; handlers had up to 18 years on the job. The trials ran in a church building divided into four rooms. Handlers were told that marijuana or gunpowder scent might be present in up to three spots per room. In reality, no actual target scent was ever placed anywhere. Each room carried two decoy cues: a red paper marker, meant to plant a false belief in the handler's mind about where scent was hidden, and a food or toy decoy, meant to tempt the dog's own nose without being the trained target odor.

Eighteen teams ran all four room types twice, for 144 total searches. The result: 225 false alerts, with only 21 runs producing no alert at all — since no genuine scent existed anywhere, every alert was false. Alerts clustered roughly twice as often at the red-paper "human cue" spots as at the food-decoy "dog cue" spots, meaning the handler's belief about where scent was hidden shaped the dog's response more than anything actually interesting to the dog. The paper frames this explicitly as a Clever Hans effect, named for the early-1900s horse that appeared to solve arithmetic but was actually reading unconscious cues from its trainer's posture.

The study didn't appear in the Supreme Court's 2013 Florida v. Harris ruling on drug-dog probable cause, though it was cited in a defense amicus brief there. A 2011 Chicago Tribune analysis separately found suburban police dog alerts were wrong 56% of the time overall. Follow-up work has complicated the original picture: a 2020 Frontiers in Veterinary Science study found handler knowledge changed dogs' search behavior but didn't clearly change false-alert rates, and a later Lit-led study found a third of mock jurors would convict on a dog alert alone.
