# The Insurance Industry's House-Burning Lab

**The find:** The insurance industry funds a 90-acre facility in Richburg, South Carolina where researchers build full-scale houses and then deliberately burn, hail-cannon, and hurricane-wind them to find out exactly what makes a home catch fire.
**The surprise:** Houses almost never ignite from a wall of flame — they catch because wind-blown embers, traveling over a mile ahead of the fire, land on small, overlooked fuel sources (mulch, a wood fence, a vent) yards from the walls.
**The idea:** Small, unnoticed details at the point of contact decide catastrophic outcomes, not the scale of the threat itself.

## In depth

The Insurance Institute for Business & Home Safety (IBHS) is a nonprofit research center created and funded by the property-insurance industry, with a 90-acre campus in Chester County, South Carolina, about 45 minutes from Charlotte. Its centerpiece is a six-story wind tunnel roughly 145 feet square, lined with a wall of 105 fans — each nearly six feet across with a 350-horsepower engine — capable of recreating hurricane winds up to 130 mph, wind-driven rain, hailstorms fired from a hail cannon, and full wildfire ember storms, all aimed at full-scale one- and two-story houses built inside the chamber.

NPR's Planet Money visited the facility for a November 2026 episode, describing scientists setting fire to entire structures — decks, sheds, and complete houses, 13 to 14 burned to date — purely to learn which materials and construction choices survive and which don't. An Associated Press account of one side-by-side test describes two identical houses, built in the same spot weeks apart: one with cedar siding, bark mulch, and vinyl gutters was fully engulfed within three minutes of wind-whipped embers reaching it; the other, built with cement siding, gravel instead of mulch, and metal gutters, survived the same ember exposure untouched. A related 2018 Headwaters Economics report found that building the fire-resistant version costs roughly the same as the flammable one — the difference is specification, not budget.

IBHS president Roy Wright describes the approach bluntly: "We crash test houses." The underlying finding that keeps recurring across the institute's wildfire experiments is that most home ignitions don't come from direct flame contact with a wall — embers travel more than a mile ahead of a fire front and accumulate in small, overlooked vulnerable spots: vents, gutters, mulch beds, the base of a wooden fence touching the house, the gap under a deck. IBHS's research has already shaped California's building code, which now requires ignition-resistant walls, tempered double-pane windows, and mesh screening over vents on new construction in fire-prone areas — regulatory changes that trace back to a lab built specifically to watch houses burn down on purpose.
