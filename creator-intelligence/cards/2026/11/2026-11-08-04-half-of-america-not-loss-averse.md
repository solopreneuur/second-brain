# Half of America Isn't Loss Averse

**The find:** Economists Chapman, Snowberg, Wang and Camerer ran an incentivized, nationally representative survey of 3,000 US adults and found only about 50% are "loss averse" in the textbook sense — versus the 70-90% loss-averse rate assumed from decades of lab studies run on college students.
**The surprise:** One of behavioral economics' most load-bearing "universal" findings turns out to be an artifact of who gets studied, not a fact about people in general.
**The idea:** [Loss aversion](../../../concepts/loss-aversion.md) correlates with cognitive ability and education, so lab populations (students) systematically overstate how loss-averse "people" really are.

## In depth

Loss aversion — the idea that losing something hurts roughly twice as much as gaining the equivalent amount feels good — has been one of the most cited findings in behavioral economics since Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky introduced it in 1979. Nearly all the foundational evidence came from lab experiments on convenient samples, usually university students, where 70 to 90 percent of participants showed the pattern.

Jonathan Chapman, Erik Snowberg, Stephanie Wang and Colin Camerer set out to check whether that held in the general population. Using a method they call Dynamically Optimized Sequential Experimentation (DOSE) — a faster, more accurate way of eliciting individual-level risk and loss preferences than older multiple-price-list surveys — they ran three separate incentivized, nationally representative surveys of US adults, combining to roughly 3,000 people, including a wave fielded by YouGov in early 2020. Across all three, only about 50 percent of respondents turned out to be loss averse; the rest were "loss tolerant," meaning they were willing to accept gambles with a negative expected value specifically because they contained the possibility of a loss, when a loss-averse person should shy away from exactly that kind of bet. In the matched student samples the researchers also tested, loss aversion rates were far higher — in the 16 to 32 percent loss-tolerant range, consistent with the old lab literature.

The paper's most striking secondary finding is what explains the gap: loss tolerance correlates with lower cognitive-ability scores, and loss-tolerant individuals were more likely to report recent gambling activity and recent financial shocks. In other words, the "irrational" loss-tolerant group isn't randomly distributed — it's concentrated among people who are already more financially stressed, suggesting loss attitudes aren't a fixed personality trait so much as something shaped by circumstance. The authors frame this as support for a broader point: people do value gains and losses differently, but the specific, textbook-sized asymmetry assumed in decades of downstream economic modeling was calibrated on exactly the wrong population.
