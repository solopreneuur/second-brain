# Treating yourself makes you generous

**The find:** Across seven experiments with nearly 2,800 people, those who freely chose their own small indulgence (chocolate, a fun video) later volunteered 54% more time and donated more than twice as much to charity than people given the identical treat but told to take it.
**The surprise:** It isn't the pleasure that makes people generous afterward — it's the act of choosing the pleasure for yourself.
**The idea:** [Self-compassion licensing](../../../concepts/self-compassion-licensing.md) — deliberately choosing a small kindness toward yourself signals "I am cared for," and that signal spills over into caring for others.

## In depth

A 2026 paper in the International Journal of Research in Marketing by Cristian, Fennis, Mukhopadhyay and Warlop set out to separate two things that are normally tangled together: the pleasure of a treat, and the act of choosing it. Across seven experiments involving almost 2,800 participants, one group was told to pick their own small indulgence — a piece of chocolate, a short funny video, a few minutes of a favorite song — while a matched group was simply handed the identical treat and told to consume it. The treat itself, and its pleasure, were held constant; only the presence or absence of personal choice varied.

Afterward, participants in both groups were given a chance to be generous: volunteer time for a tedious task, or donate money to a charity. People who had chosen their own treat volunteered roughly 54% more time for the boring task, and donated more than twice as much money, than people whose identical treat had simply been assigned to them. The effect wasn't about having had a nice experience — it reliably disappeared in control conditions where the "treat" was something low-pleasure (plain rice crackers) or where the generous ask itself became burdensome (volunteering a full hour instead of a few minutes), suggesting a bounded, specific mechanism rather than a generic good mood.

The researchers' explanation is that choosing to indulge yourself functions as a small act of self-care — proof, to yourself, that you're worth being treated well. That inferred sense of being cared for appears to generalize outward: having just demonstrated that you deserve kindness, you become measurably more willing to extend kindness to others. It reframes "treat yourself" messaging, long used purely as a self-indulgence pitch, as something with a real downstream effect on prosocial behavior — a brand encouraging a small deliberate self-reward may be setting up its customer to be more generous shortly afterward, not less.
