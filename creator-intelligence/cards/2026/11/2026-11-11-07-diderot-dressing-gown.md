# A new robe wrecked a philosopher's house

**The find:** The philosopher Denis Diderot got a beautiful new scarlet dressing gown as a gift in 1769. His old one had been perfectly comfortable — but next to the new gown, his chair, desk, and prints suddenly looked shabby, so he replaced them all to match, piece by piece, and went into debt doing it.
**The surprise:** Diderot wrote the essay himself: "I was absolute master of my old dressing gown, but I have become a slave to my new one" — one nice object quietly obligates you to upgrade everything around it.
**The idea:** [The Diderot effect](../../../concepts/diderot-effect.md) — one new possession that breaks from your existing set doesn't just sit there, it pulls everything around it toward a pricier match.

## In depth

In 1769, the French philosopher Denis Diderot — editor of the Encyclopédie — received a gift: a beautiful new scarlet dressing gown. The old one, worn thin and stained with ink, had been perfectly comfortable. The gift came after a favor Diderot had done for the Paris salon hostess Marie-Thérèse Rodet Geoffrin, who repaid him by replacing his shabby furnishings, starting with the gown. Diderot described the fallout in an essay titled "Regrets sur ma vieille robe de chambre" ("Regrets on Parting with My Old Dressing Gown," subtitled "A Warning to Those Who Have More Taste Than Fortune"). Wearing the new scarlet robe, he noticed everything around it now looked wrong: his straw chair looked cheap next to it, so he replaced it with an armchair upholstered in Moroccan leather. His old writing desk looked shabby, so he bought an expensive new writing table. His prints no longer suited the room, so he swapped them for pricier ones more in fashion, and hung a mirror above the mantelpiece to complete the look. Piece by piece, his study was made over to match the gown, and he went into debt doing it. He summarized the irony directly: "I was absolute master of my old dressing gown, but I have become a slave to my new one." The old robe had let him work and live without worry; the new one came with upkeep and anxiety about ruining it.

Two centuries later, the anthropologist Grant McCracken, trained at the University of Chicago and later a Harvard Business School researcher, picked up Diderot's anecdote in his 1988 book "Culture and Consumption: New Approaches to the Symbolic Character of Consumer Goods and Activities." McCracken named the pattern the "Diderot effect" and built it into a theory of "Diderot unities" — clusters of possessions a culture reads as belonging together. Introducing one object that breaks from an existing unity, McCracken argued, pulls the surrounding objects toward a new, pricier unity to restore coherence, driving sustained spending rather than a single purchase.

Writers since have mapped the same mechanic onto new territory: a sleek glass cabinet making mismatched kitchen dishware look wrong and triggering a full pantry replacement; one renovated room making the adjacent room look dated by comparison; smartphone upgrades cascading into cases, earbuds, and watches from the same brand; and e-commerce interfaces engineered to manufacture the same mismatch-and-complete urge Diderot described in his own study.
