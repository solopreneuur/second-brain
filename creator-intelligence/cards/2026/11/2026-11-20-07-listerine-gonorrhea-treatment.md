# Listerine was sold as a gonorrhea cure

**The find:** Chemist Joseph Lawrence invented Listerine in 1879 as a surgical antiseptic. For its first ~35 years it was marketed as a floor cleaner, a dandruff treatment, and a cure for gonorrhea and diphtheria — only becoming a dentist-recommended mouthwash in 1895 and finally a mass-market "bad breath" product on drugstore shelves in 1914.
**The surprise:** The product now synonymous with fresh breath spent decades being sold as a treatment for a sexually transmitted infection.
**The idea:** [Category repositioning](../../../concepts/category-repositioning.md) — the exact same formula can be sold on wildly different jobs-to-be-done for decades until one framing finally sticks and erases the others from memory.

## In depth

Joseph Lawrence, a chemist in St. Louis, developed Listerine in 1879 and named it after the British surgeon Joseph Lister, the pioneer of antiseptic surgical technique, because the formula's original purpose was as a surgical disinfectant — something to clean wounds and instruments at a time when germ theory was only just becoming accepted medical practice. Lawrence licensed the formula to the Lambert Pharmaceutical Company, and for the next several decades Listerine was sold as a general-purpose antiseptic with an extraordinarily wide range of claimed uses: it was marketed at various points as a floor cleaner for hospitals, a treatment for dandruff, a cure for foot odor, and — most strikingly by modern standards — a treatment for gonorrhea and diphtheria, two serious bacterial infections, decades before antibiotics existed as a real alternative.

The pivot toward dentistry began modestly: in 1895, the Lambert company pitched Listerine to dentists as a surgical antiseptic they could use and recommend in their practices, and it slowly built a reputation in that professional channel over the following two decades. But the breakthrough that created the Listerine most people know today didn't happen until 1920s advertising innovation. Gerard Lambert, the founder's son, and an advertising team essentially invented — or at minimum dramatically popularized and medicalized — the word "halitosis," an obscure clinical term for bad breath, and built a now-legendary advertising campaign around the fear of having it without knowing it, aimed squarely at single, marriage-seeking adults. Sales reportedly rose from roughly $100,000 in the early 1920s to over $4 million by the end of the decade as "chronic halitosis" became a manufactured social anxiety that only Listerine could solve.

What's less told than the halitosis-marketing legend is just how long and strange the pre-history was: the same bottle of liquid spent nearly four decades being pitched as a cure for an STI, a cleaning product, and a dandruff treatment before anyone thought to sell it as a breath freshener — meaning the "obvious" modern use of Listerine wasn't obvious at all; it was one of at least five distinct positioning attempts, and the only one that happened to catch fire.
