# A bottle built to survive breaking

**The find:** In 1915, Coca-Cola's lawyer set a brief for a glassmaker competition: design a bottle "so distinct you'd recognize it in the dark, or lying broken on the ground." Root Glass Company's Earl Dean based the winning shape on a cacao-pod illustration he found at the library.
**The surprise:** The patent drawings deliberately left off the Coca-Cola logo to hide the design from rival bottlers while it was pending — but the actual bottles that reached stores were embossed with the logo from day one, debunking the popular myth that they were ever sold unmarked.
**The idea:** [Distinctive assets](../../../concepts/distinctive-assets.md) — a shape engineered to be identified by touch or by fragment, built specifically to defeat copycats who were already imitating the drink.

## In depth

In April 1915, the Coca-Cola Bottlers' Association, pushed by company attorney Harold Hirsch, authorized a competition among roughly eight to ten U.S. glass manufacturers for a bottle shape no other bottler could legally imitate. Hirsch's brief, as the company later recounted it, asked for a bottle "so distinct that you would recognize it if you felt it in the dark, or lying broken on the ground" — a direct response to dozens of soda makers (Koka-Nola, Ma Coca-Co, Toka-Cola, and others) selling look-alike drinks in look-alike straight-sided bottles.

Root Glass Company of Terre Haute, Indiana assembled a small team — plant supervisor Alexander Samuelson, auditor Clyde Edwards, and machinist/designer Earl R. Dean — to compete. Dean and Edwards went to the Emeline Fairbanks Memorial Library looking for imagery tied to Coca-Cola's ingredients (coca leaf, kola nut) and instead found an illustration of a cacao seed pod, whose elongated, ribbed shape became the basis of Dean's sketch and hand-machined mold. This is the well-documented origin — it was not an accident in the sense some retellings claim, where someone supposedly looked up the wrong word; Snopes has examined and rejected that framing. The "hobble skirt" comparison, often cited as the inspiration, is actually a nickname the bottle picked up afterward, because its narrow waist resembled the fashionable skirts of the era — a public reaction, not a design input.

Root's design beat the other submissions and was chosen by Coca-Cola executives in 1915. The design patent (D48,160, filed August 1915, granted November 16, 1915) was issued under Samuelson's name, and its drawings omitted the Coca-Cola script to keep the shape's connection to the brand confidential from competing bottlers while the patent was pending. That's distinct from the common claim that production bottles were sold unmarked: once manufacturing began in 1916, bottles were embossed with the script and a bottling-city mark from the start. The shape itself received a U.S. trademark registration in 1960, one of the first ever for a container shape, cementing its legal protection.
