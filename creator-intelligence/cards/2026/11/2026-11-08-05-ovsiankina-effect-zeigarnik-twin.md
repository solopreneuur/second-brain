# The Zeigarnik Effect's Replicating Twin

**The find:** The famous "waiter remembers unpaid orders better" idea (the Zeigarnik effect) barely replicates — a 2025 meta-analysis of 37 studies found interrupted tasks are recalled no better than finished ones — but its lesser-known lab-mate, the Ovsiankina effect, holds up strongly: people spontaneously go back to finish an interrupted task about 67% of the time.
**The surprise:** The marketing-world-famous half of the idea (open loops help you remember) is the weak half; the real, durable effect is the compulsion to finish, not better memory for what's unfinished.
**The idea:** [The Ovsiankina effect](../../../concepts/ovsiankina-effect.md): an interrupted task creates a pull to complete it that has nothing to do with memory.

## In depth

In 1927, Bluma Zeigarnik, a doctoral student in Kurt Lewin's Berlin lab, ran an experiment that became one of psychology's most-cited findings: she gave people roughly 20 short tasks (puzzles, bead-stringing, arithmetic), let half finish and interrupted the other half, then asked everyone to recall what they'd done. Interrupted tasks were recalled nearly twice as often — the origin of the now-ubiquitous claim that unfinished business lodges in memory, cited everywhere from cliffhanger TV writing to productivity advice about leaving work "open" overnight.

A year later, in the same lab, Zeigarnik's colleague Maria Ovsiankina ran a related but distinct study: rather than testing memory, she gave people free time after an interruption and simply watched whether they went back to the task unprompted. Most did. This became known as the Ovsiankina effect — not a memory phenomenon, but a behavioral one.

In July 2025, Romain Ghibellini and Beat Meier of the University of Bern published a meta-analysis in Nature's Humanities and Social Sciences Communications pooling every study they could find on both effects. The results were lopsided. For the Zeigarnik memory effect, the weighted recall ratio across 37 publications (excluding Zeigarnik's own original data, which the authors flag as inflated) came out to 0.99 — statistically no difference at all between recall of interrupted and completed tasks, and the overall effect size was small (Cohen's d = 0.15) even when a result appeared. The authors conclude the "memory advantage" is not universal and likely depends heavily on situational factors from 1927 Berlin — like the experimenter's perceived authority — that simply don't generalize.

The Ovsiankina effect told a different story. Pooling 21 publications, people resumed interrupted tasks 67% of the time when given the chance — well above the 50% you'd expect from chance alone, and the researchers call it a genuine, general tendency. In plain terms: being interrupted doesn't make you remember a task better, but it does create a nagging pull to go finish it, which is arguably the more useful insight for anyone designing an app's notification, a serialized story, or a checklist.
