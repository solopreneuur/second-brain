# "No business being where they are"

**The find:** In the early 1990s, the US cellular industry had already picked a rival standard (TDMA) over Qualcomm's CDMA technology, a Stanford professor said CDMA "violated the laws of physics," and the San Diego Union-Tribune's own coverage said bluntly that Qualcomm "have no business being where they are." Qualcomm's Irwin Jacobs kept building CDMA anyway. It became the technical foundation of 3G, and is in most smartphones today.
**The surprise:** The press didn't just doubt the bet — in print, they said the company had no business existing, after the industry had already voted against its technology.
**The idea:** [Right idea, wrong time](../../../concepts/right-idea-wrong-time.md): when a technical "law" everyone cites turns out to be an engineering assumption, not a physical constant, outlasting the consensus can be the whole strategy.

## In depth

Irwin Jacobs earned a doctorate from MIT, taught electrical engineering there and then at UC San Diego, and in 1968 co-founded Linkabit with Andrew Viterbi, building satellite and encryption systems that grew to 1,400-plus employees before a 1980 sale to M/A-COM. Jacobs left three years later and, on July 1, 1985, launched Qualcomm with six co-founders, each putting in roughly $1,500. By the late 1980s the US cellular industry had a problem: analog networks were running out of capacity, and in 1989 the CTIA's member carriers and manufacturers — AT&T, Motorola, Ericsson, Nortel — picked TDMA, standardized as IS-54, as the digital upgrade path, largely because it was closest to ready. Qualcomm, pitching a different scheme called CDMA (adapted from military spread-spectrum communications, where every call is spread across the whole frequency band simultaneously rather than sliced into time slots), was treated as a late, implausible entrant. Stanford electrical engineering professor Bruce Lusignan became CDMA's most vocal academic critic, arguing in forums and later in a 1996 LA Times piece that the scheme lacked adequate protection from signal fading and couldn't deliver the capacity gains Qualcomm claimed. Writer George Gilder later popularized the sharper framing that the objection amounted to saying CDMA "violated the laws of physics." A Wall Street Journal piece in 1996 argued Jacobs's promotion was pushing carriers to waste billions on unproven technology.

Jacobs kept building: a pivotal November 1989 demo for over 100 executives nearly collapsed when a GPS timing glitch hit the base stations, but the team recovered during a 45-minute scramble and the system worked. The TIA approved CDMA as IS-95 in July 1993. Verizon's predecessors and Sprint bet on CDMA while AT&T stuck with TDMA/GSM-style paths; South Korea's government adopted CDMA nationally in 1993 and launched the world's first commercial CDMA network on January 1, 1996, hitting over a million subscribers within the year — proof-of-scale that preceded the US rollout. Europe's GSM camp fought Qualcomm's IP during 3G standardization; Ericsson and Qualcomm settled a two-and-a-half-year patent dispute in March 1999, cross-licensing CDMA, WCDMA and cdma2000 patents and clearing the ITU's path to a WCDMA 3G standard built on Qualcomm's patented core. Qualcomm's stock then became the single best performer in the S&P 500 for 1999, up roughly 2,600%. The company's licensing arm now collects royalties on virtually every 3G/4G/5G phone sold regardless of chip supplier, generating billions annually at operating margins often above 60-70%, long after the "laws of physics" objection evaporated.
