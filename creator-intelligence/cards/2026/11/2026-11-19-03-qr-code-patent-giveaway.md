# QR codes spread because of an unused patent

**The find:** In 1992, Denso Wave engineer Masahiro Hara was asked to fix a Toyota factory problem: a single part often needed ten-plus barcodes because each one held too little data. He got the fix playing Go on his lunch break, realizing a grid of stones could encode far more than a line of bars.
**The surprise:** Denso Wave patented the QR code in 1998 and still owns that patent today — but made a public, permanent commitment never to enforce it against anyone using the standard format, which is the actual reason it ended up on every menu on earth.
**The idea:** The invention wasn't what made the format universal — the decision to retain ownership but renounce enforcement is what let a corporate-owned patent behave like an open standard.

## In depth

Masahiro Hara, a Hosei University engineering graduate born in 1957, led a small Denso Wave team tasked with solving a real factory pain point: a standard one-dimensional barcode could only hold about 13 to 20 characters, so a single automotive part often needed ten or more barcodes stuck to its box to carry all its tracking data. Workers scanned the same box repeatedly to capture every field, and a smudge of grease across any one barcode made it unreadable, forcing a manual keyboard entry and stopping the line.

The breakthrough came on Hara's lunch break. A regular Go player, he has described arranging black and white stones on the game's grid and realizing the grid itself was a straightforward way of conveying far more information than a single line could. A two-dimensional grid of black and white squares could pack over 100 times a barcode's capacity into the same space, while position markers borrowed from the same visual logic let a scanner read it from any angle, even partially damaged. The result, finished in 1994, could hold up to roughly 7,000 numeric characters.

Denso Wave patented the invention (US Patent 5,726,435, filed 1995, granted 1998) and holds it to this day, but made a deliberate, public commitment never to exercise those rights against anyone using the standardized format — the patent stayed loaded but was never fired. Japan's JIS standardized the format in 1999, and ISO/IEC 18004 followed in 2000. Adoption stayed largely confined to Japanese mobile phones through the 2000s; global ubiquity arrived only once smartphone cameras could scan QR codes natively, a shift Apple accelerated by building scanning into iOS 11's camera app in 2017, then exploded further when COVID-19 made QR contactless menus and check-ins a pandemic default in 2020. Hara still works at Denso Wave and, with his team, received the European Inventor Award in 2014.
