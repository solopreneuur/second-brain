# A coffee spill made him likeable

**The find:** Elliot Aronson had people listen to a recording of a quiz contestant who answered 92% of questions correctly, sometimes with an added clip of the contestant clumsily spilling a cup of coffee on himself near the end.
**The surprise:** Listeners rated the coffee-spiller as more likeable than the clean version of the same high scorer — but splicing the identical spill onto an average contestant's recording made that person less likeable.
**The idea:** The [pratfall effect](../../../concepts/pratfall-effect.md) — a small, visible blunder only helps once you've already proven you're good.

## In depth

Elliot Aronson ran the study at the University of Minnesota in 1966 with Ben Willerman and Joanne Floyd, built around a single recording of a student trying out for a TV quiz team. In the high-competence version, the contestant answers 92% of 20 questions correctly, speaks confidently, and lists an impressive record of past achievements. In the low-competence version, the same voice answers only 30% correctly and sounds far less sure. Each had two endings spliced on: a clean goodbye, or the sound of the contestant fumbling his chair, knocking his own cup of coffee into his lap, and swearing softly. Listeners who heard only one of the four versions rated the clumsy-but-brilliant contestant as the most likeable of all four combinations — more likeable than the same person without the spill. For the mediocre contestant, the spill made things worse: clumsiness read as further confirmation of incompetence rather than a humanizing touch. Aronson's explanation was that extreme competence creates social distance — someone right 92% of the time starts to feel superhuman and hard to relate to — and a visible, harmless blunder closes that distance by proving they're fallible like everyone else. Because the mediocre contestant had no halo to begin with, the same blunder just reads as more evidence of mediocrity. Later researchers have found the effect is real but smaller and more context-dependent than the original study suggested — it shows up more reliably in some replications than others, and depends on the blunder being minor and unrelated to the core competence being judged. It's since become a stock explanation for why audiences warm to visibly brilliant but imperfect public figures, from scientists to executives to comedians.
