# Memory Pads the Photo's Edges

**The find:** Show someone a close-up photo, then ask them to redraw or recognize it later — they consistently remember seeing more of the scene than the camera actually captured.
**The surprise:** The false memory of a wider shot forms almost instantly, within a fraction of a second, and shows up in 95% of drawings tested.
**The idea:** [Boundary extension](../../../concepts/boundary-extension.md) — memory doesn't crop the way a camera does, it pads the edges with an anticipated scene that was never there.

9 · Unfamiliar concept · Psychology · [Scholarpedia](http://www.scholarpedia.org/article/Boundary_extension)

## In depth

Helene Intraub and Michael Richardson first documented this in a 1989 study, "Wide-Angle Memories of Close-Up Scenes," published in the Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Learning and Memory. In their core experiment, 37 participants studied 20 photographs for 15 seconds each, then drew each scene from memory. Boundary extension — participants drawing a wider view than they'd actually seen, with the main subject rendered smaller and more background invented at the edges — showed up in 95% of the 133 drawings collected. Intraub and colleagues called it "the rule, rather than the exception" for close-up shots; wide-angle originals sometimes produced the opposite, boundary restriction.

Later work pinned down how fast the false memory forms. Using masked, interrupted viewing instead of drawing, Intraub and Dickinson found the effect emerges after a gap of just 42 milliseconds (1/20th of a second) between viewing and test, with effect sizes around d = 0.96 at 42ms and d = 0.78 at 250ms. A classic variant from Intraub, Bender, and Mangels (1992) used a 2-second masked interval with a boundary-adjustment task, where subjects adjusted a comparison photo's edges to match their memory — also reliably producing outward (wider) adjustments.

The effect generalizes beyond static photos and vision. Intraub, Morelli, and Gagnier (2015, Cognition) found boundary extension in haptic-only exploration (touching objects in the dark) and in bimodal visual-haptic trials, with visual study producing somewhat larger extension than haptic. Blind participants show less boundary extension than sighted or visually impaired participants. Neuroscience work by Soojin Park, Intraub, Yi, Widders, and Marvin Chun (2007, Neuron) used fMRI adaptation and found the effect localizes to scene-selective regions — the parahippocampal place area and retrosplenial cortex — but not to the object-focused lateral occipital complex, suggesting the brain specifically extrapolates spatial layout, not object identity. Researchers and journalists have cited the phenomenon in discussions of eyewitness reliability, photo cropping, and VR scene design.
