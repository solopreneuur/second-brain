# The quarter was built for vending machines

**The find:** When the US Treasury switched off silver coinage in 1965, it hired the Battelle Memorial Institute to find a replacement metal for the quarter. The deciding factor wasn't cost — the new copper-nickel coin had to match the old silver one's weight and electromagnetic signature closely enough to work in every vending machine and payphone already installed nationwide.
**The surprise:** Roughly 28 billion coins a year were already moving through machines built for silver, so the machines dictated the design of the new currency, not the Mint.
**The idea:** [Path dependency](../../../concepts/path-dependency.md) — an installed base of hardware can lock in a design choice long after the original reason for it is gone.

## In depth

In 1963 the Treasury Department hired Battelle Memorial Institute, a research organization in Columbus, Ohio, to find a substitute for the silver in US dimes and quarters. Silver was becoming too expensive to coin: its price had climbed from 38.3 cents an ounce in 1943 to over a dollar by 1961, and world silver consumption more than doubled between 1958 and 1965 while mine production rose only about 15%. The Treasury capped its sale price to industry at $1.29 an ounce, but once the open-market price passed roughly $1.38 an ounce it became profitable to melt circulating dimes and quarters for their metal, so people were pulling silver coins out of circulation, worsening a coin shortage.

Battelle's brief wasn't just "find a cheaper metal." The Philadelphia Mint struck experimental pattern coins in fifteen candidate metals for dimes, seventeen for quarters, and four for half dollars, ranging from 50/50 silver-copper blends to copper-nickel-clad copper. Battelle delivered its report on February 12, 1965. The deciding factor was compatibility with the installed base of coin machinery: by 1963, Battelle found, about 28 billion coins a year were passing through vending machines in the US, with another 4 to 5 billion going through Bell System payphones alone. Any new coin had to match old silver coins in weight, diameter, and electromagnetic behavior, or it would jam or get rejected by every slot built around silver's conductivity. Coin validators of the era read a coin's eddy-current signature — a coil generates an oscillating magnetic field, the coin passing through it induces eddy currents that brake it in proportion to its conductivity and mass, and the machine accepts or rejects the coin based on whether that signature falls inside narrow preset limits. Copper-nickel-clad copper passed close enough to silver's signature to need no machine modifications nationwide, and clad quarters entered circulation on November 1, 1965.

The same compatibility problem reappeared with the UK's 1971 decimalisation, which required adjusting or replacing more than 5 million vending machines, and with Britain's 2017 twelve-sided one-pound coin, which forced an upgrade of roughly 500,000 machines at an estimated £32 million cost.
