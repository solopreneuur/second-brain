# Burger King's CEO published his number

**The find:** In a 2026 Oscars ad, Burger King's US/Canada president Tom Curtis went on camera admitting the burgers were getting squished, service was slow, and quality had slipped, then "fired" the King mascot on screen and published his own cell phone number, inviting complaints directly.
**The surprise:** A fast-food turnaround campaign that is itself a confession, delivered by a named executive handing out his real phone number on national television.
**The idea:** Public admission of failure as a credibility signal ([Radical honesty](../../../concepts/radical-honesty.md)) — owning the complaint before the customer voices it disarms it.

## In depth

Burger King had spent years losing ground to McDonald's and Wendy's on speed and consistency, and its parent Restaurant Brands International had already tried stunts (the 2020 "Moldy Whopper") to signal it would talk about quality honestly. In 2026, instead of another glossy campaign, Tom Curtis, the chain's US/Canada president, used a national Oscars ad slot to list the company's own failures on camera: squished burgers, slow service, inconsistent quality. He then had the Burger King mascot "fired" on screen and closed by reciting his personal cell number, 305-874-0520, telling viewers to call or text him directly if the company fell short again.

The stunt generated more than 86,000 calls and texts in a short window — an unfiltered customer-complaint channel routed straight to a named executive rather than a call center or a social-media intern. Curtis and his team used that feedback to drive real operational changes: reworked kitchen procedures, a revamped Whopper recipe, and tighter store-level quality checks. The results showed up fast. US comparable sales rose 5.8% in Q1 2026 against an industry average of 3.2%; by Q3 2026 BK's comps were up 8.4–8.5% while McDonald's managed only 0.8% industry-wide. Sales of the revamped Whopper rose roughly 20%.

The mechanism isn't contrition for its own sake — it's that a specific, named person taking ownership of a specific, checkable failure reads as more trustworthy than a vague "we hear you" statement from a brand account. Customers who called Curtis directly got a human answering for the company's mistakes, which made the promised fix feel real rather than performative. It's the same logic behind why apologies that name exactly what went wrong outperform generic ones, just run at national-ad-budget scale with a real phone number as the proof of commitment.
