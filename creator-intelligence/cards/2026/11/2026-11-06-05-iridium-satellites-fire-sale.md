# A $5bn satellite network's fire sale

**The find:** Motorola spent roughly $5 billion building Iridium's 66-satellite global phone network on a 12-year-old business plan. It launched into a world with cellphones already everywhere, got about 55,000 of the 1 million subscribers it needed, and filed Chapter 11 within a year. Liquidators then bought the entire system for $20-25 million, and it turned profitable needing only 60,000 customers.
**The surprise:** The exact same satellites and phones that bankrupted a $5 billion bet turned profitable at a fraction of the price, once freed of the original debt.
**The idea:** [Right idea, wrong time](../../../concepts/right-idea-wrong-time.md): the same technology can fail and succeed depending only on what it's financed against.

## In depth

Motorola began developing Iridium's business plan in the mid-1980s, before cellular phones existed in any meaningful form, and that plan stayed essentially frozen for the next twelve years while Motorola built it. The pitch was simple: a business traveler could carry one phone that worked anywhere on Earth, routed through 66 low-orbit satellites rather than ground towers. Motorola spent roughly $5 billion on the system between 1990 and 1998, plus about $1 billion in first-year operating costs once it went live. It launched commercially in November 1998. By then, the world the plan was built for no longer existed: terrestrial cellular networks had spread across Europe, Asia, and American cities, so the executives Iridium was designed for already had cheap, reliable phones in Paris and London. Iridium's phone cost about $3,000, needed a direct line of sight to the sky, so it didn't work indoors or in cars, and cost $3 to $8 per minute to use. Motorola needed roughly one million subscribers to make the economics work; it got about 55,000. On August 13, 1999, two days after defaulting on $1.5 billion in loans, Iridium LLC filed Chapter 11. Service was shut off and the satellites faced deorbiting.

In late 2000, Dan Colussy, a retired aviation and telecom executive, put together a group of investors — Syncom, Saudi Prince Khalid, Brazil's Inepar, and Australian-backed Quadrant — and bid $25 million for the entire system: the satellites, ground network, and control infrastructure, with $6.5 million in cash and $18.5 million in convertible debt. A bankruptcy court approved the sale in November 2000. Stripped of Motorola's $5 billion in sunk costs and debt service, the identical hardware only needed about 60,000 subscribers to break even — roughly what it already had. The reorganized Iridium Satellite LLC relaunched service in 2001 and became profitable.

Today, as Iridium Communications, the company has roughly 2.6 million billable subscribers, spanning government, military, maritime, aviation, and IoT customers. It holds a seven-year, $738.5 million fixed-price U.S. Space Force contract guaranteeing roughly $110.5 million a year in revenue regardless of usage. In June 2026, Rocket Lab agreed to acquire Iridium for about $8 billion, a deal expected to close in mid-2027.
