# Researchers flipped moral licensing into honesty

**The find:** Portsmouth Business School researchers asked survey respondents about good, everyday habits — whether they have a Netflix subscription, whether they go to the cinema — right before asking how much digital piracy they had done. Respondents who got the "good deeds" questions first admitted to 42% more piracy than a control group asked the piracy question cold.
**The surprise:** Moral licensing is normally described as a trap — do something good, then behave worse. Here the researchers used the same mechanism to produce a better outcome: people didn't pirate more, they just admitted to piracy they were already doing.
**The idea:** The moral credit people "bank" from a reminder of their own good behavior doesn't have to be spent on new bad behavior — it can just as easily be spent on telling the truth about old behavior ([Licensed confession](../../../concepts/licensed-confession.md)).

## In depth

Kate Whitman and Zahra Murad of the University of Portsmouth's Portsmouth Business School, with co-authors Joe Cox and Aldert Vrij, published "License to Spill: The Influence of Moral Licensing Survey Primes on Deviant Behavior Reporting" in the journal Deviant Behavior in December 2025, building on an earlier 2023 Portsmouth working paper that piloted the same technique.

The design targets a known problem in survey research: people underreport embarrassing or illegal behavior, especially digital piracy, because admitting it threatens their self-image as a law-abiding person. Respondents first answered a short block of questions about positive, commonplace activities that support creative industries — a Netflix or Spotify subscription, going to the cinema — on a simple agreement scale, before being asked to estimate how much they had illegally downloaded or streamed in the past week. A control group went straight to the piracy questions with no priming block. In the 2023 precursor study, the positive-cues priming treatment increased the amount of piracy respondents were willing to report by 42% relative to the unprimed control — a large jump in self-reported deviance caused entirely by the order and content of the preceding questions, not by any change in actual behavior.

The effect was not uniform. Respondents who scored high on social-desirability scales, meaning they were especially motivated to look good, showed the largest honesty gains, because they had the most underreporting to recover from — the prime removed some of the image threat that was suppressing their answers most. Gender mattered too: women, who on average score higher on social desirability and underreport piracy more at baseline, showed a particularly strong boost in honest reporting once primed with their own good deeds.

The authors explicitly invoke moral licensing, the mechanism from research such as Nina Mazar and Chen-Bo Zhong's green-products studies, where recalling or performing a virtuous act buys psychological permission to indulge afterward. Classic moral licensing is framed as a trap: cheat on a diet, donate less, litter more, because you have already banked enough moral credit. This study repurposes the exact same mechanism as a measurement tool: the "indulgence" here is not a new bad act, it is simply the willingness to admit, on paper, to a bad act the person was already doing. The moral credit gets spent on self-honesty rather than on new misbehavior. The authors suggest the technique could improve self-report accuracy for other underreported behaviors — tax evasion, workplace misconduct, health-risk behaviors — anywhere survey designers currently fight social-desirability bias with cruder tools like anonymity assurances.
