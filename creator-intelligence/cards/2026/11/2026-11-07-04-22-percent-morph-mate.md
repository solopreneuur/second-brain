# Your face is hiding in your partner's face

**The find:** Researchers digitally blended a small slice of each subject's own face into a photo of their real-life partner. The blend rated most attractive wasn't 0% or 33% — it centered on 22% self-resemblance, beating a version blended with an independently-rated "ideal" face. Subjects never consciously detected their own face in the photo.
**The surprise:** People unknowingly find their partner most attractive when the partner's face carries a little of their own in it, and they can't tell why or that it's happening.
**The idea:** [Mere exposure effect](../../../concepts/mere-exposure-effect.md): we're implicitly drawn to a narrow, unconscious sweet spot of self-similarity, not to maximum objective beauty.

## In depth

In 2013, University of Oslo psychologist Bruno Laeng, with Oddrun Vermeer and Unni Sulutvedt, published "Is Beauty in the Face of the Beholder?" in PLOS ONE. They recruited 20 heterosexual couples (40 people, average age 28, together at least two years), photographed each partner, and used morphing software to blend small percentages of one partner's own face into a photo of their real-life partner, altering only the internal features (eyes, nose, mouth region) so the base face stayed clearly recognizable. Each participant then ranked seven morphed versions of their partner's face: a 22% self-blend, blends with an average male or female "prototype" face, a blend with an androgynous prototype, blends with independently-rated "best" composite faces, and a mirror-symmetry version. Across both male and female participants, the 22% self-blend was ranked most attractive, beating the prototype and "best face" morphs by wide margins. A control group of unrelated raters, shown the same images with no personal stake, ranked those same self-blended photos dead last — confirming the effect depended on the viewer's own face being secretly present, not on some neutral quality of the blend.

A second experiment tested three resemblance doses (11%, 22%, 33%) on 10 of the couples: 22% again won; 11% and 33% dropped to the bottom, suggesting a specific sweet spot rather than "more is better." A third experiment used a forced-choice detection task and found participants performed no better than chance at noticing their own face had been blended in — the preference operated entirely unconsciously. The researchers framed this as evidence for "positive assortative mating": people are implicitly drawn to phenotypic self-similarity in mates, a pull theorized to run through imprinting-like, mere-exposure, and kin-recognition mechanisms, but kept in check by an opposing pressure to avoid incest-like pairings, producing a moderate rather than maximal resemblance optimum. The same lab followed up in 2014 by blending participants' own faces versus their opposite-sex parent's face into attractiveness-rated composites, finding the self-based standard dominated over a parental-imprinting standard. A 2016 study found the self-resemblance preference appeared mainly in partnered people, not singles, who instead preferred dissimilar faces. Separately, real-world assortative-mating research has repeatedly shown raters can match real spouses' photos to each other above chance, indicating actual couples resemble each other facially more than random pairings, independent of any "growing to look alike" over time.
