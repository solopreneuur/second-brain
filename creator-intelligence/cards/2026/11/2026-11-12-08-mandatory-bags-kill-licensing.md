# A mandatory tote bag kills its own loophole

**The find:** Karmarkar and Bollinger's grocery study found reusable-bag shoppers bought more indulgent, junk-food items than other shoppers. The fresh detail: when a store mandated reusable bags instead of leaving them optional, that effect completely disappeared, according to Bollinger.
**The surprise:** The loophole isn't in the bag, it's in the choosing. Remove the choice and the "I've been good, I can treat myself" permission vanishes along with it.
**The idea:** Moral licensing only operates when the virtuous act feels like your own decision, not a rule imposed on you ([Moral licensing](../../../concepts/moral-licensing.md)).

## In depth

Uma Karmarkar and Bryan Bollinger's widely cited 2015 paper, "BYOB: How Bringing Your Own Shopping Bags Leads to Treating Yourself and the Environment," analyzed real grocery-store transaction data and found that shoppers who brought reusable bags were 13% more likely to buy organic food and 7% more likely to buy indulgent items like chips, cookies, and ice cream, compared to shoppers using disposable bags. The interpretation offered was moral licensing: the voluntary, visible act of bringing your own bag functions as a small virtuous deposit that buys permission to spend it on something less virtuous in the same trip.

The detail that didn't make it into most retellings of the study concerns what happens when the choice disappears. In markets or periods where reusable bags became mandatory by law or store policy rather than a personal choice, Bollinger reports the licensing effect "completely went away." That single sentence does more explanatory work than the headline finding. It means the mechanism isn't really about the bag as an object, or even about the behavior of reducing plastic waste. It's specifically about the subjective experience of having chosen to do something good, voluntarily, that then grants permission to indulge. A mandated version of the same behavior produces no such credit, because there was no choice to feel virtuous about.

This has a direct, practical edge for anyone designing a loyalty program, wellness incentive, or sustainability initiative: if the goal is to avoid licensing backsliding (people cashing in good behavior for bad behavior elsewhere), making the virtuous behavior mandatory rather than opt-in removes the psychological reward that licensing runs on — at the cost of also removing whatever goodwill voluntary participation might have generated. The tradeoff is built into the mechanism itself.
