# Liquid Death sponsored a fictional death show

**The find:** For Paramount's 2025 "The Running Man" remake, Liquid Death became the official in-universe beverage sponsor of the movie's fictional, lethal game show, with an in-film commercial where Colman Domingo's character pitches the water brand ("bury your thirst in a shallow grave"). Paramount approached Liquid Death, not the other way around.
**The surprise:** The joke of the brand (canned water that looks and markets like an energy drink built for a death cult) became literal: a movie studio treated Liquid Death as the only plausible real-world sponsor for a show where losing means dying.
**The idea:** A brand built entirely on one consistent, legible joke becomes easy for outside partners to cast correctly, without explanation ([Distinctive assets](../../../concepts/distinctive-assets.md)).

## In depth

Liquid Death's branding has always leaned on the gap between its product (canned still and sparkling water) and its presentation (heavy-metal fonts, skulls, death-themed copy). That consistency is what let a major studio recruit it as a sponsor inside a piece of fiction rather than through a traditional ad buy. Paramount's "The Running Man," based on the Stephen King novel about a deadly televised game show, wrote Liquid Death into the film itself as the show's in-universe beverage sponsor, the same way real game shows carry a real beverage sponsor in the background.

Per the brand's VP of Creative Andy Pearson, the pitch logic was: "What would Liquid Death do if the world of The Running Man actually existed... sponsor this brutal, bloodthirsty game show, the same way other beverage brands sponsor The Voice or American Idol." Colman Domingo's character performs an in-film commercial for the brand, and the joke works precisely because audiences already know Liquid Death's real marketing voice well enough to read the fictional version as barely exaggerated.

The mechanism generalizes beyond stunt marketing: a brand with a narrow, unmistakable identity is legible to outside creators without a briefing document. A studio, another brand, or a creator can slot it into a new context and trust the audience will immediately understand the reference, because the brand's single joke has been repeated consistently enough to become shorthand. Most brands have to explain themselves in every new partnership; a sufficiently distinctive one gets cast correctly by people who've never spoken to its marketing team.
