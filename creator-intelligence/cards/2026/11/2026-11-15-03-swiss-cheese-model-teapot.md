# A teapot full of cat food

**The find:** Psychologist James Reason built the "Swiss Cheese Model," now the standard framework for investigating plane crashes, hospital deaths and cyberattacks, after absent-mindedly spooning cat food into his own teapot instead of tea leaves in the early 1970s.
**The surprise:** He didn't treat the slip as embarrassing — he treated it as data, started cataloguing everyday absent-minded errors, and built a career-defining theory out of the pattern.
**The idea:** The [Swiss Cheese Model](../../../concepts/swiss-cheese-model.md): accidents happen when the holes in several independent layers of defense happen to line up at once.

7 · Far-field · Psychology · [Flight Safety Australia](https://www.flightsafetyaustralia.com/2025/02/the-absent-minded-professor-who-made-a-safer-world/)

## In depth

In the early 1970s, James Reason, a psychology lecturer at the University of Leicester, was making tea when his cat — "a very noisy Burmese" — howled to be fed. He opened a tin of cat food, dug in a spoon, and dolloped it into the teapot instead of the cat bowl. Reason recognized this as a "capture error": a strongly practiced motor sequence hijacked by habit while his conscious attention was elsewhere. Rather than dismiss it, he started systematically collecting diary records of everyday slips and lapses from himself and others, building a taxonomy of absent-minded error that became the empirical base of his 1990 Cambridge University Press book "Human Error." He later retold the teapot story in his 2013 memoir "A Life in Error: From Little Slips to Big Disasters," describing how "this bizarre slip would change my professional life."

"Human Error" introduced Reason's "defense in depth" model: accidents happen when latent organizational weaknesses line up with active, individual unsafe acts across multiple layers of a system. The now-iconic name came from someone else: Rob Lee, then head of Australia's Bureau of Air Safety Investigation, looked at Reason's diagram of stacked defensive layers with shifting gaps and remarked it looked like Swiss cheese — he later demonstrated the idea on video by holding up slices of Emmental. The illustrated version is also credited to Reason's collaborator Dante Orlandella. Australia gave the model its first real-world test: the Bureau of Air Safety Investigation applied it in the 1993 Monarch Airlines accident inquiry, and it was later folded into ICAO's Annex 13 accident-investigation standards.

Today the model underpins root-cause analysis in aviation safety boards, surgical-error and patient-safety reviews, and cybersecurity's "defense in depth" architecture, where each control layer is one porous cheese slice and breaches occur only when the gaps align.
