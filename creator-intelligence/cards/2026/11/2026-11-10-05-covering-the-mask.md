# The mask almost everyone wears

**The find:** Legal scholar Kenji Yoshino's concept of "covering" — toning down a known trait rather than hiding it — explains FDR's waist-up-only photos and Margaret Thatcher's voice lessons to erase her accent, and a Deloitte survey found 45% of straight white men also report doing it.
**The surprise:** Covering isn't a minority-identity problem — almost half of the group assumed to have nothing to hide admitted doing it too, for things like age, religion or mental health.
**The idea:** [Covering](../../../concepts/covering.md) — you can know someone's full identity and still demand they turn the volume down on it, a different, subtler demand than forcing them to hide it outright.

## In depth

Sociologist Erving Goffman first used "covering" in his 1963 book Stigma, to describe how people with a known, visible difference still manage how much of it shows. NYU and Yale law professor Kenji Yoshino picked up and expanded the term in his 2006 book "Covering: The Hidden Assault on Our Civil Rights," drawing partly on his own experience as a gay man who, even after coming out, still modulated how "gay" he let himself seem at work. Yoshino's key move is separating covering from passing: passing hides a trait entirely, while covering lets a trait be known but demands it be downplayed. His go-to historical example is Franklin D. Roosevelt, whose paralysis from polio was public knowledge, but who was almost never photographed in his wheelchair and was always arranged to appear standing or seated behind a desk for public appearances — the disability was known, but its visibility was managed. Margaret Thatcher is a similar case: her working-class Grantham accent was never secret, but she took voice coaching to lower her pitch and soften it before becoming party leader. The idea stayed mostly academic until Deloitte's 2013 "Diversity's New Frontier" survey quantified it at scale: 61% of a large US workforce sample said they covered some aspect of their identity at work, and critically, 45% of straight white men — the demographic with the least obvious reason to — said the same, most commonly around age, mental health, family circumstances, or religion. That finding reframed covering from a minority-rights issue into a broader workplace-culture one: Yoshino argues companies that focus only on banning overt discrimination can still run cultures that quietly demand everyone mute something true about themselves, and that authenticity, not just non-discrimination, is the harder and more useful goal.
