# Pronounceable stock tickers beat the market

**The find:** Princeton researchers compared NYSE/AMEX stocks by how pronounceable their 3-letter ticker codes were. A $1,000 investment split across the most pronounceable tickers beat the same bet on the least pronounceable ones by $85, after just one day of trading.
**The surprise:** Investors were measurably swayed by how a meaningless three-letter code sounded in their heads, not by anything about the business behind it.
**The idea:** [Cognitive fluency](../../../concepts/cognitive-fluency.md): things that are easier to process get judged more favourably, even a ticker symbol.

## Deeper · 2026-11-02

The find began as a quirky lab demonstration before it ever touched real trading data. Adam Alter and Daniel Oppenheimer, psychologists then at Princeton, first asked ten undergraduates to rate the pronounceability of 60 made-up stock names, then had a separate group of students play a stock-picking game using nothing but those names — fluent-sounding fictitious companies got bought more and were predicted to perform better, with no information about the underlying "business" at all. Having shown the bias existed in the lab, Alter and Oppenheimer then went looking for it in real markets. For their core ticker-symbol study, they pulled NYSE and AMEX-listed firms from 1990 to 2004, winding up with usable return data on 665 NYSE and 116 AMEX stocks. Two independent raters, resolving disagreements by discussion, simply judged whether each three-letter ticker (things like KAR versus RDO) could be pronounced "according to the laws of English pronunciation" — no formula, just human coding. They then computed what $1,000 split evenly across the most pronounceable tickers would have earned versus the same $1,000 split across the least pronounceable ones, at four points after a stock started trading: one day, one week, six months, and one year. The headline number is the one-day gap: $85.35 more profit for the fluent basket, significant at p<0.05 on the NYSE and marginally so on AMEX (p=0.09). The edge shrank fast but didn't vanish — $42.40 at one week, $37.10 at six months, $20.25 at one year — a pattern consistent with a short-lived mispricing that markets partly, but not fully, arbitrage away. A parallel study using full company names rather than ticker codes, on 89 NYSE stocks from the same 1990–2004 window, found an even larger and longer-lived effect: fluent-named firms beat disfluent ones by $112 after one day, $118 after one week, $277 after six months, and $333 after one year, with the longer-horizon gaps reaching significance too. Alter and Oppenheimer published both studies together in PNAS in June 2006 as "Predicting Short-Term Stock Fluctuations by Using Processing Fluency," framing the mechanism as the fluency heuristic: ease of processing produces a subtle positive affective response that gets misread as a signal about the thing itself — tickers and names carry no business information, so the only thing distinguishing KAR from RDO is how it feels to read.

The ticker study sits inside a much larger research program on processing fluency that both authors built out independently. Oppenheimer's most famous solo contribution is "Consequences of Erudite Vernacular Utilized Irrespective of Necessity," a 2006 paper (which won him an Ig Nobel Prize) showing that writers who use needlessly long, complex words get rated as less intelligent by readers, not more — fluency of the prose, not the vocabulary, drives the judgment of the author's mind. This plugs into a well-established "fluency heuristic" literature going back decades: Rolf Reber and Norbert Schwarz showed in the late 1990s that statements printed in high-contrast, easy-to-read fonts or colors get rated as more true than identical statements in low-contrast fonts, and a widely cited demonstration found that a recipe printed in an ugly font was judged to take roughly 36 minutes to prepare versus about 23 minutes for the same recipe in a clean font. The broader "more fluent equals more true" effect is called the illusory truth effect, first documented in 1977 by Lynn Hasher, David Goldstein, and Thomas Toppino, who found that merely repeating a statement across sessions, true or false, raised people's rated belief in it — repetition breeds fluency, and fluency gets misread as validity. Fluency has since been linked, across dozens of studies, to judgments of liking, safety, value, and skill, all from stimuli that are easier on the eye or ear to process.

The ticker finding did not go unchallenged once other researchers tried to extend it. Durham and Santhanakrishnan (2016) found the fluency effect on tickers is conditional, not universal: in periods following high investor sentiment, fluent-ticker stocks actually underperformed disfluent ones, the opposite of the original pattern, suggesting sentiment regime matters as much as pronounceability. Separate work on modern, highly liquid, algorithm-dominated large-cap US markets found no statistically or economically meaningful ticker-fluency effect at all (coefficient near zero, p=0.495), implying that where arbitrage is fast and cheap, the mispricing Alter and Oppenheimer caught in 1990–2004 data gets priced away before it shows up. The effect has, however, resurfaced convincingly outside the US: Fang and Zhu (2019) found that Chinese-market stocks with more complex ticker/name codes are held by fewer investors, trade less, and earn lower post-IPO returns, with the effect concentrated among retail-heavy, information-poor stocks — exactly where a cognitive shortcut would be expected to matter most. Related firm-level work (Green and Jame, and others) found that fluent company names correlate with broader shareholder bases, higher trading volume, and higher valuation multiples, and extended the logic to actual IPOs, where firms with more pronounceable names or tickers show higher first-day returns. Even Oppenheimer himself was notably cautious about over-reading his own result, reportedly telling reporters he wouldn't advise anyone to trade on it and that it was unclear how robust the pattern really was, given it came from one slice of market history.

Fluency's reach into money and judgment extends well past tickers. Oppenheimer and collaborators have run studies on how font and layout fluency shift perceived task difficulty and persuasiveness; marketers routinely exploit the same logic in pricing displays (simpler-looking prices, cleaner typefaces, and shorter numeric strings are judged as cheaper or more trustworthy) and in brand-naming. What's robust across this literature: fluency reliably shifts snap judgments of truth, liking, and competence in controlled lab settings, and the illusory truth effect itself has replicated many times. What's contested: how large and durable these effects are once real money, real arbitrageurs, and real market efficiency enter the picture — the original ticker-pronounceability finding looks like a genuine but fragile and context-dependent short-term mispricing rather than a stable, exploitable market inefficiency, stronger in inefficient or retail-dominated markets and weaker or reversed in liquid, sentiment-driven, or algorithmically arbitraged ones.
