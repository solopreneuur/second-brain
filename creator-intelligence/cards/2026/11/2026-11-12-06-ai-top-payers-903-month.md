# 1% of AI subscribers spend $903 a month

**The find:** a16z's October 2026 consumer AI report measured actual spend, not downloads or traffic, for the first time. Roughly half of US adults use an AI tool, but only 4.5% have an active paid subscription. Among the people who do pay, the median payer spends $25 a month, and the top 1% of payers spend $903 a month.
**The surprise:** Usage and revenue are almost completely decoupled. Half the country touches the product; a tiny sliver pays anything; an even tinier sliver inside that sliver accounts for revenue far out of proportion to their numbers.
**The idea:** Mass adoption and a viable business can be two unrelated facts about the same product, and the real economics live inside the smallest, least visible slice of the user base.

## In depth

Andreessen Horowitz's "Top 100 Gen AI Consumer Apps" series has previously ranked apps by usage and web traffic. The October 2026 edition did something different: it measured real consumer spending data on AI subscriptions as of August 2026, rather than proxies like downloads or time-in-app. The result reframes what "AI is everywhere" actually means commercially.

About half of US adults report using some AI tool, consistent with the widely repeated adoption narrative. But only 4.5% of US consumers have an active paid subscription to a product like ChatGPT, Gemini, or Claude. Among that paying minority, spending is heavily skewed: the median paying user spends about $25 a month, roughly the price of a single standard subscription tier. The top 1% of payers, by contrast, spend an average of $903 a month — more than thirty-five times the median.

That whale-economics pattern explains decisions that otherwise look strange from the outside. It's why a company with hundreds of millions of free or lightly-engaged users still experiments with advertising to monetize the broad base, while simultaneously rolling out premium tiers priced at $100-plus a month aimed squarely at the small group of power users who are already spending far more than the median subscriber. The free-to-paid funnel isn't one business, it's effectively two: a volume business built on a huge, mostly non-paying base, and a tiny high-value business nested inside it, extracting outsized revenue from power users whose needs and price sensitivity look nothing like the median user's. The same shape shows up in other digital products with a hard-core segment (mobile games, trading apps, streaming), but the AI numbers make the skew unusually explicit this early in the category's life.
