# Thorp smelled Madoff's fraud in 1991

**The find:** In spring 1991, a client hired mathematician and hedge-fund pioneer Ed Thorp to vet a fund that invested through Bernie Madoff's "split-strike conversion" strategy. Thorp checked roughly 160 of Madoff's reported options trades against real exchange records and found no record of about half of them.
**The surprise:** Thorp told the client to pull out and warned people in his network for years afterward — seventeen years before Madoff's 2008 confession, one person doing the actual arithmetic had already proven the fund was a fraud.
**The idea:** Checking a handful of claimed transactions against the public record is [decisive evidence](../../../concepts/decisive-evidence.md) — it beats trusting reputation, length of track record, or who else is invested, which is exactly what let Madoff run for another seventeen years on everyone else.

## In depth

Ed Thorp made his name twice over before 1991: first as the mathematician who proved blackjack could be beaten with card counting (published as *Beat the Dealer* in 1962), then as the quant who co-founded Princeton Newport Partners, one of the earliest and most successful hedge funds, running from 1969 to 1988 with only three losing months in nineteen years. After Princeton Newport closed amid an unrelated prosecutorial raid on a sister firm, Thorp kept trading privately and consulting.

In spring 1991, a client asked Thorp to evaluate a fund that claimed remarkably smooth, consistent returns by using a "split-strike conversion" options strategy run through a broker named Bernard Madoff. Thorp did what almost no one else bothered to do: he pulled the fund's claimed trades and tried to match them against actual recorded prices and volumes on the exchanges where they supposedly happened. Of about 160 trades he checked, roughly half had no corresponding record at all — they simply hadn't happened as described. The fund's reported performance was mathematically impossible given its stated strategy; the smoothness itself was a tell, since no legitimate strategy with that profile could avoid losing trades as rarely as Madoff's did.

Thorp advised his client to withdraw immediately and spent years afterward telling people in his circle that Madoff was running a Ponzi scheme — well before Harry Markopolos's more famous whistleblowing campaign to the SEC in the early 2000s, and seventeen years before Madoff's sons turned him in to the FBI in December 2008, when the fraud was revealed to have cost investors an estimated $65 billion on paper.

What makes Thorp's catch notable isn't access to secret information — it's that he treated an extraordinary claim as something to verify against a primary record rather than evaluate by reputation, tenure, or the credibility of other investors already in. Thorp, still alive and public at 93, has written and spoken about the episode periodically as a case study in why audited, checkable claims should always outrank unaudited consistency.
