# The algorithm that removed "too much empathy"

**The find:** RealPage's rent-pricing software YieldStar, built by ex-Alaska Airlines pricing scientist Jeffrey Roper, pulled pricing decisions away from local leasing agents and gave them to an algorithm instead. Roper told ProPublica why: "We said there's way too much empathy going on here... This is one of the reasons we wanted to get pricing off-site."
**The surprise:** Landlords weren't automating away a cost center, they were automating away their own staff's sympathy for renters — training materials told property managers to accept the algorithm's price at least 90% of the time.
**The idea:** Empathy tax ([Empathy tax](../../../concepts/empathy-tax.md)): when a company says it's "taking the emotion out of" a customer-facing decision, the emotion was probably the only thing capping the price.

## In depth

In October 2022, ProPublica reporter Heather Vogell published an investigation into YieldStar, a rent-setting algorithm sold by the Texas company RealPage to landlords nationwide. Its architect, Jeffrey Roper, had a history: in the 1980s and 1990s he was director of revenue management at Alaska Airlines during an era when federal agents investigated the airline industry for coordinating fares through shared pricing data — agents once removed a computer and documents from Roper's own office. RealPage hired him in 2004 to run what was then M/PF YieldStar. The software works by having landlord clients upload their own confidential lease data — rents actually signed, concessions offered, occupancy and renewal rates — into a shared pool with competitors nearby. An algorithm then recommends a daily price per unit designed to maximize revenue across a landlord's whole portfolio, sometimes advising a vacant unit be held empty longer rather than discounted. Roper told ProPublica the quiet part about why pricing was centralized: "We said there's way too much empathy going on here... This is one of the reasons we wanted to get pricing off-site," explaining that local leasing agents tended to discount out of sympathy for renters. Training materials instructed property managers to accept the suggested price nearly every time — one Greystar document told staff to approve at least 95% of RealPage-generated prices.

RealPage's reach is vast: its pricing tools have touched close to 20 million rental units nationally. The story set off a cascade of litigation. The DOJ, joined by ten states, sued RealPage in August 2024, later adding landlord co-defendants including Greystar, Blackstone's LivCor, and Camden Property Trust. RealPage settled with the DOJ in November 2025 with no monetary penalty but agreed to stop using real-time competitor data and accept a three-year court-appointed monitor. Washington D.C.'s attorney general separately won settlements including $9.3 million from two landlords. Estimates of the rent effect vary: ProPublica documented buildings where rent rose as much as 42% since 2012, while the White House Council of Economic Advisers estimated algorithmic pricing cost renters roughly $70 a month on average.
