# Martha Stewart took a pay cut to stay relevant

**The find:** Martha Stewart filmed a Halloween ad for Liquid Death and released a $58 "luxury candle" shaped like a severed hand gripping one of its cans, for what Liquid Death's founder says was roughly a tenth of her usual fee, well over $1 million. His stated reason for her doing it: "She knows it makes her relevant to be a part of Liquid Death."
**The surprise:** The usual celebrity-endorsement economics run one direction, the brand pays for the star's reach. Here the star effectively pays, in discount, to borrow the brand's relevance instead.
**The idea:** Cultural relevance can flow from an upstart brand to an established name, not just the reverse, once the upstart has enough of the attention the legacy name is short on ([Status illusion](../../../concepts/status-illusion.md)).

## In depth

Liquid Death's founder and CEO Mike Cessario has described the arithmetic behind the brand's Martha Stewart collaboration in blunt terms: Stewart, an 84-year-old lifestyle mogul who commands over $1 million for a typical brand partnership, did this one for roughly a tenth of that rate. His explanation wasn't modesty on her part, it was strategy: "she knows it makes her relevant to be a part of Liquid Death."

The campaign itself, "Martha's Handmade Halloween," cast Stewart in a 60-second spot and paired it with a real retail product, a $58 candle shaped like a wax human hand clutching a Liquid Death tallboy, sold under the tagline "Dismembered Moments." It's consistent with Liquid Death's established playbook of taking an incongruous, respectable figure and running them through its skulls-and-death branding completely straight-faced, rather than winking at the audience.

What makes the Stewart deal worth isolating from Liquid Death's other celebrity stunts is the reversal in who is buying what. In a standard endorsement, the brand is purchasing access to the celebrity's existing audience and credibility; the celebrity's fee reflects the size of that borrowed audience. Here, by Cessario's own account, Stewart's camp accepted a steep discount because proximity to a brand that is unusually good at capturing youth attention and cultural conversation functions as its own currency, worth trading fee for. It's a reminder that "relevance" itself behaves like a tradeable asset that can flow in either direction between an establishment figure and an upstart, once the upstart has built up enough of the specific kind of attention the establishment figure is short on.
