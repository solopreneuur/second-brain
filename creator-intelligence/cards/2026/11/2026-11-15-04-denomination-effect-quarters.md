# Quarters spend differently than dollars

**The find:** Given four quarters, 63% of people in a 2009 study spent the dollar on vending-machine candy; given the exact same dollar as one bill, only 26% did.
**The surprise:** It's the identical amount of money — breaking it into small change flips a mental switch from "savings" to "pocket money," and spending it then triggers a "what-the-hell" overspend of whatever's left.
**The idea:** The [denomination effect](../../../concepts/denomination-effect.md): the same money spends differently depending on what size bills or coins it's held in.

## In depth

The denomination effect, named by Priya Raghubir (NYU Stern) and Joydeep Srivastava (University of Maryland) in a 2009 Journal of Consumer Research paper, showed that people treat identical sums of money differently depending on what denomination they're holding. The paper ran three field studies. Study 1: 89 undergraduates at two US universities were given $1 each — some as four quarters, some as a single bill — then given the chance to buy candy from a vending machine. 63% of the quarter-holders spent the money versus only 26% of the bill-holders. Study 2: 75 real customers at a gas station in Omaha, Nebraska, were paid $5 for a short survey, split across conditions (five $1 bills, five $1 coins, or one $5 bill); those holding five $1 bills were most likely to spend some in the attached convenience store, while $1-coin holders spent least, which the authors attributed to people treating coins as souvenirs rather than currency. Study 3: 150 Chinese housewives were given either a single 100-yuan note or five 20-yuan notes and asked about household purchases; those with the single large note reported less willingness to spend and less satisfaction spending it.

Raghubir and Srivastava's explanation centers on mental accounting and fungibility: large bills feel like a sealed, singular unit — "real money" earmarked for saving — while small bills and coins get mentally bucketed as disposable "pocket cash," lowering the psychological cost of parting with them.

Helen Colby (then at UCLA, later Indiana University) extended the finding in "Don't Break the $100 Bill," showing a $100 bill curbed spending more than five $20s, and documenting the "what-the-hell effect": once a large bill gets broken for one purchase, the remaining change loses its "whole" status and gets spent faster and more completely than if it had started as small bills. Later work (the "denomination–spending matching effect," 2021) found spending is fastest when note size matches price size. Applications: casinos deliberately convert cash to chips to exploit "payment decoupling"; Coinstar's cash-to-coin-conversion fee nudges people toward spending coins differently than bills; retailers and tip-pooling systems favor small bills to loosen spending.
