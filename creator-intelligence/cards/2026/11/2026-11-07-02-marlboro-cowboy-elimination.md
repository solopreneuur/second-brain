# The Marlboro cowboy won a real elimination round

**The find:** In December 1954, Leo Burnett's team brainstormed how to relaunch Marlboro as a masculine cigarette. The room threw out cab driver, sailor, marine, pilot and race car driver before someone said "cowboy," and the table agreed instantly. Philip Morris nearly killed it — research found only about 3,000 full-time cowboys in the whole US — but the ad ran anyway, alongside sea captains, weightlifters and war correspondents as a rotating cast of "masculine" figures.
**The surprise:** The enduring icon wasn't chosen by elimination in the pitch meeting. It was one guess among several that all ran in parallel for years, and the market, not the room, picked the winner.
**The idea:** [Reframing](../../../concepts/reframing.md): a single enduring icon often starts as one of several ideas run side by side; the real elimination happens in public, not in the brainstorm.

## In depth

In December 1954, on a Saturday before Christmas, Leo Burnett invited his creative staff out to his farm south of Chicago to solve a specific problem: Philip Morris's Marlboro, a filtered cigarette sold since 1924 as a women's brand with the slogan "Mild as May" and a red "beauty tip" designed to hide lipstick stains, held well under 1% of the US cigarette market and needed to relaunch as a man's cigarette alongside a new flip-top box. Burnett asked the room a simple question: "What is the most masculine figure in America?" The answers came fast — cab driver, sailor, marine, pilot, race car driver — and then someone said "cowboy," and the table agreed almost instantly. Copywriter Draper Daniels supplied the line that would run under the first ad: "Delivers the goods on flavor." Burnett already had an image in mind: a 1949 Life magazine photo essay by Leonard McCombe on Clarence Hailey "C.H." Long, a 39-year-old foreman on the 320,000-acre JA Ranch in the Texas panhandle. The idea nearly died before it launched — Philip Morris commissioned research that found only about 3,000 full-time working cowboys existed in the entire United States, and executives questioned how office workers in Manhattan were supposed to relate to one. Burnett argued his way past the objection, and the cowboy ad ran in January 1955, making Marlboro the top-selling cigarette in greater New York within 30 days.

The cowboy was never meant to be the whole campaign — just the opening entry in a planned rotation of "masculine confidence" types: sea captains, weightlifters, war correspondents, construction workers, sailors, pilots, scientists, farmers, and tattooed regular guys, several played by agency and Philip Morris staff themselves. That mixed cast ran through the mid-to-late 1950s under the jingle "You get a lot to like — filter, flavor, flip-top box." But the numbers separated the cowboy from the pack: Marlboro sold roughly 18 million cigarettes in 1954, then 6.4-6.7 billion in 1955, 14.3 billion in 1956, and 19.5 billion in 1957 — about three times as many cigarettes in a single day as in all of 1954. By 1958, with 4.5% US market share, the tattooed-men and other non-cowboy figures were quietly dropped, and Burnett's team concentrated entirely on cowboys — later, after 1963, actual working cowboys and rodeo riders set to Elmer Bernstein's "Magnificent Seven" theme. Market share climbed through the following decades: roughly 10% by 1970, the #1-selling US brand by 1972, and around 40% of the US market by 2017 — the longest-running and most successful brand mascot in advertising history, run until 1999.
