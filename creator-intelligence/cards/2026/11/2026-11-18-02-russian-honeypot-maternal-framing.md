# A Russian honeypot's maternal framing

**The find:** Aliia Roza, a former Russian intelligence "honeypot" operative, tells host Steven Bartlett on Diary of a CEO: "I look at humans as a mother and they're all 3 years old." When he responds, "It sounds like empathy," she doesn't correct him.
**The surprise:** A professional manipulator describes her targets using the language of nurturing motherhood, not contempt or calculation.
**The idea:** Expert influencers often frame control as care — a "caretaker lens" that lets manipulation feel, even to the manipulator, like kindness ([Empathy as tactic](../../../concepts/empathy-as-tactic.md)).

## In depth

The clip comes from a Diary of a CEO episode released in mid-November 2026, built around the resignation of Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, who stepped down on November 13, 2026 after reports linked him to a suspected Chinese intelligence honeypot operation. Host Steven Bartlett brought on two guests to unpack the mechanics: John Kiriakou, a former CIA officer who spent years running counterterrorism operations before publicly blowing the whistle on the agency's torture program, and Aliia Roza, who describes herself as a former Russian operative trained in psychological manipulation and seduction tradecraft.

Roza's "mother and they're all 3 years old" line comes while she's explaining how she approached targets during operations — not as equals to be respected or marks to be despised, but as something closer to dependents to be managed. When Bartlett offers the word "empathy" as an interpretation, she lets it stand, suggesting that in her own mind the caretaking frame and the manipulation were not in tension. Kiriakou, elsewhere in the same episode, adds a complementary claim: that the CIA deliberately recruits people with sociopathic tendencies (short of full sociopathy, which would fail the agency's polygraph screening) because the trait is operationally useful.

Together the two claims describe a consistent psychological pattern in high-stakes influence work: professionals who exert serious control over other people tend to describe what they do in prosocial terms — care, guidance, even love — rather than domination. That reframing isn't necessarily conscious deception of the audience; Roza's answer reads as a sincere description of her own internal experience. The same mechanism is visible in far more ordinary contexts, from manipulative management styles that get described as "mentorship" to manufactured urgency in sales get framed as "looking out for the customer."
