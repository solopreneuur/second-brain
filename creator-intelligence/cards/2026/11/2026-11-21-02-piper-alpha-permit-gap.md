# Two Permits, One Missing Valve

**The find:** On Piper Alpha, a North Sea oil platform, a safety valve was pulled for maintenance and sealed with a hand-tightened cover under one permit-to-work; a separate permit let the nightshift restart the pump it belonged to, because the two permits were filed by location and never cross-referenced.
**The surprise:** Both jobs were individually signed off and "compliant" — the explosion that killed 167 people came from two correct paperwork trails that never spoke to each other.
**The idea:** [Fragmented authorization](../../../concepts/fragmented-authorization.md) — locally valid, globally blind sign-offs. It travels to shift handovers, approval chains, and "who owns this ticket" workflows everywhere.

## In depth

On the night of July 6, 1988, Piper Alpha, a North Sea platform 120 miles off Aberdeen, exploded and burned, killing 167 of the 226 men aboard. That evening, Pump A — a condensate injection pump — had its pressure safety valve (PSV 504) removed for routine overhaul. The open pipework was sealed with a blind flange, hand-tightened rather than bolted, as a temporary measure. This was recorded on a permit-to-work, which was left in the control room when the dayshift ended. A second, separate permit authorizing restart of Pump A existed but did not reference the missing valve; because permits were filed by location rather than cross-linked, the nightshift crew — needing to replace a failed Pump B under rising gas demand — found only the pump's permit, not the valve's. At 9:55 pm they started Pump A. Without its safety valve, pressure built past what the loose flange could hold, and gas began leaking audibly, triggering gas alarms. The leak ignited around 10:00 pm, blowing out a wall between the module housing Pump A and the control room, killing the control-room crew and disabling the platform's firefighting command. At 10:20 pm the Tartan platform's gas riser, pressurized to roughly 120 atmospheres, failed in the fire, releasing an estimated 15-30 tons of gas per second that ignited immediately; at 10:50 pm the MCP-01 riser ruptured too. Compounding the disaster, the platform's firewater pumps had long been switched from automatic to manual to stop them drawing in divers working subsea — meaning no one could, or did, start them once the fire began. Piper Alpha collapsed into the sea within about two hours.

Lord Cullen's 1990 public inquiry found the permit-to-work system "totally inadequate," poorly audited, and routinely bypassed through informal practices rather than rigorous training. His 106 recommendations led directly to the Offshore Installations (Safety Case) Regulations 1992, requiring operators to demonstrate that major hazards are reduced to ALARP (as low as reasonably practicable), and to the 1997 industry-wide "Step Change in Safety" initiative.
