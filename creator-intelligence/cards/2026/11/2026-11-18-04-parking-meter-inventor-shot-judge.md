# The inventor who shot a judge

**The find:** Carl Magee, the Oklahoma City toolmaker credited with inventing the parking meter in 1935, spent the previous decade as a muckraking New Mexico newspaper editor whose reporting helped crack open the Teapot Dome oil-lease scandal — and he once shot a judge in a hotel lobby and was tried for manslaughter.
**The surprise:** The parking meter is the least dramatic thing Carl Magee ever did.
**The idea:** A person's most famous invention can be the epilogue to a much bigger first act, not the headline of their life.

## In depth

Carlton Cole "Carl" Magee (1873–1946) trained as a teacher and lawyer before buying the Albuquerque Morning Journal in the early 1920s. As editor, he pursued the Teapot Dome affair — the scheme in which Interior Secretary Albert B. Fall secretly leased federal oil reserves to private operators without competitive bidding — with a ferocity that made him a target. New Mexico's political establishment, much of it tied to Fall's allies, fought back with a string of libel suits, contempt-of-court citations, and at least one manslaughter charge against Magee over the following years; his newspaper was effectively run out of business by legal harassment at one point. Fall was ultimately convicted and briefly imprisoned, the first cabinet secretary jailed for crimes committed in office, and historians credit Magee's reporting as a key factor in forcing the federal investigation that got there.

The judge-shooting incident, in 1926, grew directly out of this fight. Magee had repeatedly clashed in court with Judge David J. Leahy (sources vary on the exact name recorded), and the conflict escalated into a physical confrontation in the lobby of Albuquerque's Hilton Hotel. In the scuffle, a pistol discharged; the bullet missed the judge but struck a bystander, attorney John Brinkley, who later died of the wound. Magee was tried for manslaughter and acquitted on grounds of self-defense — a verdict contemporary accounts describe as deeply controversial in a town where Magee had plenty of enemies.

Battered by the lawsuits and the fallout from the shooting, Magee relocated to Oklahoma City around 1927, where he later turned a civic traffic problem — merchants complaining that all-day parkers were starving downtown of turnover — into the coin-operated parking meter, patented in 1935 and first installed that July. He co-founded the Magee-Hale Park-O-Meter Company to manufacture it; it is still one of the most litigated-over, widely copied inventions in American civic infrastructure.
