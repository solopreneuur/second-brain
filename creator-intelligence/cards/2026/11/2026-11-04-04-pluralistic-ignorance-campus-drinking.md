# Nobody actually liked the drinking

**The find:** Princeton students privately rated themselves much less comfortable with campus drinking culture than they believed the average student was; over a semester, men's own attitudes drifted toward the norm they wrongly assumed others held.
**The surprise:** The group looked unanimous about something that, privately, almost nobody in it actually believed.
**The idea:** [Pluralistic ignorance](../../../concepts/pluralistic-ignorance.md): a norm can persist and spread purely because everyone mistakenly thinks everyone else endorses it, not because anyone actually does.

9 · Unfamiliar concept · Psychology · [JPSP, 1993](https://psycnet.apa.org/doi/10.1037/0022-3514.64.2.243)

## In depth

Pluralistic ignorance was named by social psychologist Floyd Allport, who studied the mismatch between private belief and perceived public opinion at Syracuse University in the 1920s. He described the idea — what he called the "illusion of universality of opinions" — in his 1924 book *Social Psychology*, then gave it its lasting name in *Students' Attitudes* (1931), co-written with his student Daniel Katz. Katz had found that most white fraternity members privately had no objection to admitting Black students, yet because each member believed he was in the minority, the fraternities kept voting to exclude them.

Seven decades later, Princeton psychologists Deborah Prentice and Dale Miller ran the clearest demonstration of the mechanism. In "Pluralistic Ignorance and Alcohol Use on Campus" (*JPSP*, 1993), they asked Princeton undergraduates how comfortable they were, personally, with campus drinking practices, and how comfortable the average student was. Across the first two studies (over 300 students combined), the average student rated their own comfort around 5.3 on an 11-point scale but rated the typical peer's comfort just above 7 — even though actual private comfort levels among students were statistically indistinguishable from each other. Everyone thought they were the outlier.

The sharpest finding came from a 25-student panel surveyed in September and again eight to nine weeks later. Men's attitudes and reported drinking shifted measurably toward the comfort level they (wrongly) attributed to their peers — they drank more and said they were more at ease doing so. Women showed no such shift; instead, the persistent gap was associated with rising feelings of alienation from campus life. A follow-up by Schroeder and Prentice (1998) found that students told the truth about the gap drank significantly less months later. The same mechanism has been invoked for the bystander effect and for Hubert O'Gorman's 1975 finding that white Americans in the late 1960s badly overestimated how many other white Americans still supported segregation, helping the policy outlive the private consensus against it.
