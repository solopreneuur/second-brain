# Radiologists missed the gorilla

**The find:** Researchers digitally pasted a gorilla image — 48 times the size of an average lung nodule — into a lung CT scan and asked 24 expert radiologists to search it for nodules, as they normally would.
**The surprise:** 83% never reported seeing the gorilla, and eye-tracking showed most of them had looked directly at it for over half a second.
**The idea:** [Inattentional blindness](../../../concepts/inattentional-blindness.md) doesn't shrink with expertise — trained experts can stare straight at something and still not consciously register it, because their attention is tuned to a specific task, not to everything in the frame.

## In depth

In 2013, Harvard researchers Trafton Drew, Melissa Võ, and Jeremy Wolfe published "The Invisible Gorilla Strikes Again: Sustained Inattentional Blindness in Expert Observers" in Psychological Science. They recruited 24 radiologists — nine from Brigham and Women's Hospital, fifteen American Board of Radiology expert examiners, mean age 48 — and had each freely scroll through five lung CT scans, up to three minutes per scan, searching for cancer nodules exactly as they would in real practice. On the fifth and final scan, they digitally inserted a small gorilla image, about 48 times the volume of an average lung nodule, visible across five consecutive CT slices.

Twenty of the 24 radiologists — 83% — never reported seeing the gorilla. Eye-tracking showed that 12 of those 20 had looked directly at its location while it was on screen, with an average fixation time of 547 milliseconds — long enough for visual processing, but not for conscious recognition. On average, radiologists scrolled back through the gorilla-containing slices 4.3 times and spent nearly six seconds total looking at them.

The study is a deliberate homage to Simons and Chabris's 1999 "invisible gorilla" experiment, where people counting basketball passes in a video often missed a person in a gorilla suit walking through the scene. The key difference: that original study used novices on an artificial counting task. This one tested trained experts doing their actual job, showing the blindness persists despite years of domain expertise — contradicting earlier research suggesting expertise reduces the effect. The researchers coined the term "sustained inattentional blindness" because, unlike the original gorilla video's brief exposure, the radiologists viewed the anomaly repeatedly over several seconds. They also ruled out "satisfaction of search" — finding one target making you less likely to notice others — as the explanation.
