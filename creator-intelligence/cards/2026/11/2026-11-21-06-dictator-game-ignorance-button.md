# The Button Nobody Wants Pressed

**The find:** In a money-split game, researchers gave people a free, costless button that would reveal exactly how their choice affected the other player's payout before they committed.
**The surprise:** 44% refused to press it — and those who stayed deliberately blind took the selfish split far more often than the people who looked.
**The idea:** [Strategic ignorance](../../../concepts/strategic-ignorance.md), also called "moral wiggle room" — people don't avoid bad behavior, they avoid the information that would make bad behavior feel bad.

2 · Shocking number · Psychology · [Economic Theory, 2007](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00199-006-0153-z)

## In depth

Dana, Weber, and Kuang set up a binary dictator game at Carnegie Mellon and the University of Pittsburgh. A "dictator" chose between Option A, paying themselves $6 and the anonymous "receiver" $1, or Option B, paying both people $5. In the transparent baseline, 14 of 19 dictators (74%) chose the fair $5/$5 split. The researchers then added a hidden-information treatment: 32 dictators faced the same choice, but the computer did not initially tell them which option paid the receiver $1 and which paid $5 — the amounts were scrambled between two possible payoff tables. A button, free and immediate, would reveal which table was in play before they committed. Fourteen of the 32 dictators (44%) chose not to press it, deciding blind. The other 18 (56%) looked. Once the consequences were visible, most lookers still chose fairly — only 15 of the 32 total dictators both revealed the payoffs and picked the fair option, meaning the overwhelming majority of those who avoided the button took the selfish $6/$1 split. Overall, generous behavior fell from 74% in the transparent condition to under half once dictators could dodge the information, even though looking cost nothing and changed no incentive.

The paper's name for this is "moral wiggle room": people act generously when fairness is unambiguous, but given a costless excuse to not know, many take it and then act selfishly. Published as Jason Dana, Roberto A. Weber, and Jason Xi Kuang, "Exploiting Moral Wiggle Room: Experiments Demonstrating an Illusory Preference for Fairness," Economic Theory, vol. 33, no. 1 (2007), the design has since been replicated and extended into charitable giving and ambiguity in donations, cementing "strategic ignorance" and "willful ignorance" as standard terms in behavioral economics for how people protect their self-image by not looking.
