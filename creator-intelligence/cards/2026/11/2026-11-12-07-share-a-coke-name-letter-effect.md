# A 1980s lab finding sold a billion Coke bottles

**The find:** Psychologist Jozef Nuttin documented in 1985 that people have a measurable unconscious preference for the letters in their own name over other letters, used mainly as a lab test for implicit self-esteem. In 2011, Coca-Cola Australia put first names directly on bottles in its "Share a Coke" campaign and drove double-digit sales increases, before the idea spread to over 80 countries.
**The surprise:** A quiet, decades-old finding about why people like their own initials, built to measure something as abstract as self-esteem, turned out to be a complete blueprint for one of the most successful bottle redesigns in beverage history.
**The idea:** People have an unconscious pull toward their own name, and putting it on a product is often enough to turn a commodity into something that feels personally made ([Name-letter effect](../../../concepts/name-letter-effect.md)).

## In depth

Jozef Nuttin, a Belgian psychologist, first documented the name-letter effect in a 1985 paper, running a simple test across 11 European countries: show people the full alphabet and ask them to rate how much they like each letter, then compare the ratings for the letters that happen to be in their own name against the ratings everyone else gives those same letters. Across every country and writing system tested, people rated the letters in their own name more favorably than non-namesake people rated those same letters. Nuttin used this as an unobtrusive measure of implicit self-esteem: because people aren't consciously choosing to favor their own initials, the bias has to be coming from somewhere automatic, and it has since been replicated widely and used in research on self-liking, decision-making (there's evidence people are more likely to choose brands, careers, or even partners whose names resemble their own), and voting behavior.

For roughly 25 years, the finding stayed inside academic psychology. Coca-Cola Australia's 2011 "Share a Coke" campaign is the clearest commercial translation of it: the company replaced its logo on bottles with the 150 most common first names in Australia, inviting people to find a bottle with their own name, or a friend's, and share it. The campaign is widely credited with reversing a decline in Coca-Cola's sales among younger drinkers in Australia that year, and it was expanded into dozens of other countries over the following several years, each with localized name lists, becoming one of the most studied and copied packaging campaigns of the 2010s.

The mechanism the campaign activates is close to a direct, literal application of Nuttin's lab result: a mass-produced, identical product becomes personally meaningful the instant your own name appears on it, triggering the same unconscious self-liking bias Nuttin measured with nothing more than letter-rating surveys. It's a useful case of a narrow academic finding, built to study self-esteem in the abstract, turning out decades later to be directly actionable at a global commercial scale, with almost no modification needed to the original insight.
