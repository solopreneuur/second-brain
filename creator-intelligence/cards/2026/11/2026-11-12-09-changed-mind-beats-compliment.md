# Changing your mind beats being nice all along

**The find:** Elliot Aronson and Darwyn Linder had subjects overhear a stranger's opinion of them evolve over several meetings. People liked the stranger most when her opinion went from negative to positive, more than when it was positive the whole time — and liked her least when it went from positive to negative, more than when it was negative throughout.
**The surprise:** Consistent approval doesn't win the most affection. A reluctant convert, someone who initially didn't rate you and then warmed up, ends up liked more than someone who liked you from the start.
**The idea:** A changing opinion carries more evidential weight than a static one, because it reads as something earned rather than a given.

## In depth

Elliot Aronson, already known for his work on cognitive dissonance, ran this study with Darwyn Linder to test what became known as "gain-loss theory" of interpersonal attraction. Subjects had a series of staged conversations with a confederate, and were secretly allowed to overhear her evaluating them to another person after each one. Four conditions were tested: consistently positive evaluations throughout, consistently negative evaluations throughout, a shift from negative to positive (a "gain"), and a shift from positive to negative (a "loss").

The gain condition produced the highest reported liking of all four groups, higher than the consistently positive condition. The loss condition produced the lowest liking of all four, lower than the consistently negative one. In other words, ending up liked after initially being disliked beat being liked the entire time, and ending up disliked after initially being liked was worse than being disliked from the start.

Aronson's explanation was that a static opinion carries less information than a changing one. If someone likes you from the first meeting, that approval could just be who they are — maybe they like everyone, or they're simply polite. But if someone starts out unconvinced and then comes around, the later approval reads as something they arrived at through actually getting to know you, which makes it feel more credible and more valuable. The same logic works in reverse for the loss condition: losing someone's esteem after having it feels like a real verdict on you, not noise.

The practical edge for anything built on social proof or endorsement: a visibly skeptical reviewer, critic, or customer who comes around on camera is doing more persuasive work than a happy customer who was always happy, because the audience reads the shift itself as the evidence. A product's biggest fan converting from its biggest doubter is a stronger testimonial than an enthusiastic first impression, even though the second version is the one most marketing defaults to showing.

Further reading: Aronson, E., & Linder, D. (1965). "Gain and loss of esteem as determinants of interpersonal attractiveness." Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 1(2), 156-171.
