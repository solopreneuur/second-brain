# Pros putt safer to save par

**The find:** Economists analyzed 2.5 million laser-tracked PGA Tour putts and found pros sink "par" putts (where missing is a loss) at a higher rate than "birdie" putts (where missing just forgoes a gain) from the identical distance — a gap that held for Tiger Woods too.
**The surprise:** These are elite professionals, playing for huge money, with years of practice erasing the usual excuses for bias — and they still can't equalize effort between avoiding a loss and chasing a gain.
**The idea:** [Loss aversion](../../../concepts/loss-aversion.md): losses loom larger than equivalent gains, even on a golf green with nothing but skill and motivation on the line.

## In depth

In February 2011, the *American Economic Review* published a study that treated the PGA Tour as a laboratory for testing one of behavioral economics' core claims. Economists Devin Pope (University of Chicago Booth) and Maurice Schweitzer (Wharton) obtained ShotLink data — laser measurements accurate to under an inch, recorded by roughly 250 PGA Tour staff since 2002 — covering 2,525,161 putts by 421 professional golfers across 239 tournaments played between 2004 and 2009. For every putt, they knew the exact distance and whether the golfer was putting for birdie (one under par, a "gain") or par (the expected score, so missing registers as a "loss"). Controlling for distance, players made par putts about 2 to 4 percentage points more often than birdie putts from the same range; their baseline regression put the average gap at 3.6 points, rising to 3.8 points in a tournament's first round and shrinking to 2.1 points by the final round. Tiger Woods showed almost exactly the average-sized gap. After a round in 2007, Woods himself explained the asymmetry: "Any time you make big par putts, I think it's more important to make those than birdie putts. You don't ever want to drop a shot." Pope and Schweitzer also found golfers hit birdie putts less hard, leaving them short more often — a small-scale risk-aversion pattern prospect theory also predicts. They estimated that eliminating the bias would save the average pro one stroke per tournament, worth roughly $640,000 (17.6%) in extra annual earnings for 2007's top 20 money winners.

The finding mattered because it undercut a standard objection to loss aversion: that lab-demonstrated biases vanish once real money, elite skill, and years of repetition are in play. Golf offered a rare natural test — professionals are paid on cumulative strokes across 72 holes, yet par supplies an arbitrary, salient reference point on every single hole. The paper has been cited widely in behavioral economics since, including in later work on reference-dependent preferences in labor markets and incentive design.
