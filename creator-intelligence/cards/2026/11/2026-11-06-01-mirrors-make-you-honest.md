# Mirrors make you 10x more honest

**The find:** Diener and Wallbom gave students a chance to keep solving a test past a stop-bell, which counted as cheating. With no mirror in the room, 71% cheated. Facing a mirror and hearing their own recorded voice, only 7% did.
**The surprise:** Nobody else was watching — just catching your own reflection was enough to almost erase the dishonesty.
**The idea:** [Objective self-awareness](../../../concepts/objective-self-awareness.md): turning attention onto yourself as an object makes you compare your behavior against your own standards, and the gap is uncomfortable enough to close.

## In depth

In 1976, Ed Diener and Mark Wallbom at the University of Washington ran a self-awareness experiment published in the *Journal of Research in Personality* (vol. 10, pp. 107–111) under the title "Effects of Self-Awareness on Antinormative Behavior." College students were told they were taking a test of intelligence-related problem-solving (anagram puzzles) under time pressure, and were explicitly instructed to stop working the moment a bell sounded. The experimenter then left the room, giving each student the opportunity to keep solving past the bell — which counted as cheating, since continuing inflated their apparent score. Half the students worked at a desk with a mirror directly in front of them and heard a tape loop of their own voice counting; the other half had no mirror and heard someone else's voice. Of the students with no mirror, 71% kept working past the bell. Of the students facing their own mirrored image and voice, only 7% did.

The explanation Diener and Wallbom drew on was objective self-awareness theory, developed four years earlier by Shelley Duval and Robert Wicklund in their 1972 book *A Theory of Objective Self-Awareness*. The theory holds that attention can point either outward, at the environment, or inward, at the self as an object of evaluation. When a mirror, camera, or recording of your own voice turns attention onto yourself, you automatically compare your behavior against your own internalized standards — and because almost everyone falls short of their ideal selves, that comparison is aversive and pushes behavior back toward the standard, i.e., toward honesty.

The paradigm has been replicated and extended. Beaman, Klentz, Diener, and Svanum (1979) took it into the field on Halloween night in Seattle: kids told to take only one piece of candy from an unattended bowl stole at a 28.5% rate with no mirror behind the bowl, versus 14.4% with a mirror — but only when the children had already been individuated (asked their name), and only for older children. Daniel Batson's later work on "moral hypocrisy" complicated the clean story: a mirror made a coin-flip task scrupulously fair, but only when a moral standard was already salient beforehand; otherwise self-awareness just made people's stated standards drift to match whatever self-serving thing they'd already done.

The finding has since migrated into applied settings. Retail-security writers and the self-checkout industry cite mirrors mounted at checkout kiosks, angled so customers see themselves scanning, as a theft deterrent — catching your own eye while deciding whether to skip scanning an item reintroduces the self as an evaluator right at the moment temptation peaks.
