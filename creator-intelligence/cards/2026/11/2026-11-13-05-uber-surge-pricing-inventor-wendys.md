# The inventor of Uber surge pricing on Wendy's flop

**The find:** The engineer who built and ran Uber's surge pricing explained why the same mechanic triggered a consumer firestorm when Wendy's tried it on burgers in 2024.
**The surprise:** Uber's surge pricing was never really about charging more — it was a signal that pulled more drivers onto the road, because driver supply is flexible. A kitchen's fryer capacity isn't flexible, so the identical pricing lever just extracts money instead of creating supply, and reads as a scam.
**The idea:** The same mechanism can be a legitimate market-clearing tool in one domain and pure extraction in another, depending on whether the underlying supply can actually respond ([Cross-domain transfer](../../../concepts/cross-domain-transfer.md)).

## In depth

In March 2024, Kevin Novak — Uber's 21st employee, a onetime nuclear-physics grad student (his research was funded indirectly through work that fed into the Higgs boson discovery) who pivoted to the ride-hail startup and built its first surge-pricing model in roughly three weeks — published a Substack essay titled "I Invented Surge Pricing For Uber. Here's Why it Wouldn't Have Worked for Wendy's." Novak personally built Uber's entire surge system (the ML models, backend, internal ops tools, and the surge-multiplier screen riders saw in the app) starting in late 2011, when it was created specifically to handle demand spikes on Friday/Saturday nights and holidays like New Year's Eve, and he ran the team overseeing it until mid-2015. He's now founder of Rackhouse Venture Capital.

His core argument: Uber's rider-driver marketplace is "stochastic" — driver supply is not fixed but responds in real time, because drivers independently decide whether to log on and where to drive, so price signals actually pull new supply into high-demand areas. Fast-food kitchens, by contrast, have a hard capacity ceiling (grill space, fryers, staff on shift) that doesn't expand just because prices rise, so dynamic pricing can't summon more "supply" — it just extracts more money for the same burgers, which reads as pure price-gouging rather than a market-clearing mechanism.

He wrote this in response to Wendy's February 15, 2024 earnings call, where CEO Kirk Tanner said the chain would test "dynamic pricing" and "AI-enabled menu changes" as early as 2025. Media and the public read this as Uber-style surge pricing on burgers, triggering intense backlash; 12 days later Wendy's VP Heidi Schauer told NPR the idea had been "misconstrued" and said, "Wendy's will not implement surge pricing, which is the practice of raising prices when demand is highest" — reframing it as off-peak discounts instead.
