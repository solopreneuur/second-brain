# One man's shoes ran airport security for 20 years

**The find:** A single failed shoe-bomb attempt in 2001 made nearly every US air traveler take their shoes off at security for the next two decades. TSA only ended the blanket rule in July 2025.
**The surprise:** The rule didn't end because anyone decided the threat had gone away — it ended because CT scanners finally got good enough to see through shoes without removing them. The policy had outlived its own logic for years before the technology caught up.
**The idea:** A reactive patch to one specific failure can calcify into permanent policy, surviving long after the original threat model stops applying ([Path dependency](../../../concepts/path-dependency.md)).

## In depth

On December 22, 2001, Richard Reid, a 28-year-old British citizen, boarded American Airlines Flight 63, a Boeing 767 carrying 185 passengers and 12 crew from Charles de Gaulle Airport in Paris to Miami. Roughly 40 minutes after meal service, flight attendant Hermis Moutardier noticed a smoky smell, found Reid alone near a window seat trying to light a match, and saw a fuse running into his sneaker. Reid grabbed her; a second attendant, Cristina Jones, was bitten on the thumb while helping subdue him. Passengers and crew pinned Reid down with plastic handcuffs, seatbelt extenders, and headphone cords, and a doctor on board sedated him with diazepam from the plane's medical kit. FBI investigators later found about 280 grams (10 ounces) of explosives packed into hollowed shoe soles — a mix of PETN and TATP. The bomb failed because perspiration from Reid's feet had dampened the TATP in the fuse, preventing ignition. Fighter jets escorted the diverted flight to Boston's Logan Airport. Reid pleaded guilty and was sentenced to three life terms plus 110 years, and remains at the ADX Florence supermax prison.

The Transportation Security Administration, created by Congress in November 2001, did not immediately mandate shoe removal. Early walk-through metal detectors couldn't reliably detect non-metallic explosives at floor level, so TSA initially only recommended removing shoes. The rule became mandatory in August 2006 — as late as August 9 of that year TSA guidance still said shoe removal wasn't required; within about a week it became compulsory for nearly all travelers at U.S. checkpoints. Limited exemptions (TSA PreCheck, children, travelers over 75) were added starting in 2011, but the general requirement persisted for nearly two decades.

On July 8, 2025, Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem announced at Reagan National Airport that TSA would end the blanket shoes-off rule nationwide, effective immediately. DHS cited over 200 airports now equipped with CT scanners capable of 3-D imaging that can screen footwear without removal, plus "layered security" — added officers, newer screening technology, and enforcement of REAL ID starting May 2025 — as justification. Noem said: "Thanks to our cutting-edge technological advancements and multi-layered security approach, we are confident we can implement this change while maintaining the highest security standards." No new threat assessment was cited; officials framed it purely as a technology and efficiency update, with passengers still occasionally asked to remove shoes if secondary screening flags a concern.
