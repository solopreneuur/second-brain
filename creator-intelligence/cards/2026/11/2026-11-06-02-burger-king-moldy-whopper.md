# Burger King's rotting Whopper ad

**The find:** In 2020, Burger King ran a 45-second time-lapse ad showing its own Whopper rotting with mold over 35 days, to prove it had removed artificial preservatives.
**The surprise:** Deliberately showing your signature product moldy and rotting breaks every rule of food advertising.
**The idea:** [Anti-branding](../../../concepts/anti-branding.md): admitting an unflattering fact signals nothing is being hidden, which sells harder than a polished claim.

3 · Wrong decision that worked · Marketing · [WARC](https://www.warc.com/en/article/burger-king:-moldy-whopper-07931606aeef4dc988e71232613c735e)

## In depth

Burger King unveiled "The Moldy Whopper" in February 2020, using a 45-second stop-motion time-lapse to show its own flagship burger decaying over 34 to 35 days — bread browning, lettuce wilting, patty growing a thick coat of green and gray fuzz — accompanied by the line "The Beauty of No Artificial Preservatives." It ran as film, out-of-home posters, and print, timed to Burger King's announcement that it had stripped artificial preservatives, colors, and flavors from the Whopper across most of Europe and select U.S. markets. The creative was an unusual cross-agency collaboration: INGO Stockholm led creative development, working alongside David Miami and two Publicis Groupe offices in Romania and Spain — a rare instance of competing holding companies co-producing one global campaign for a single client.

The strategic logic was to make an abstract ingredient claim ("no preservatives") viscerally provable: if a burger can rot naturally, it isn't full of chemicals designed to prevent that. Rather than hide the embarrassing side effect of reformulation — faster spoilage — Burger King foregrounded it as proof of honesty, an anti-branding move: admitting an unflattering fact signals nothing is being hidden, which is more persuasive than a polished claim alone.

Per WARC's effectiveness case study, the campaign drove a 14% sales increase, a 26% lift in perception of high-quality ingredients, and a 48% increase in awareness that Burger King had removed artificial preservatives. Other tracking cited roughly 8.4 billion earned media impressions, with about 88% of resulting press coverage positive. It won the Cannes Lions Grand Prix for Creative Effectiveness, 18 Lions overall, the D&AD Black Pencil, and One Show's Best of Show, making it one of the most awarded campaigns of 2020.

Reception was genuinely split rather than uniformly positive: audience research found the ad evoked strong "gross" and "disgusting" reactions, with roughly a quarter of viewers saying it made them less likely to buy, and a majority of social mentions skewing negative in tone — even as the core message landed clearly. No reported legal or regulatory action followed. The format became a reference point for "ugly truth" honesty advertising broadly.
