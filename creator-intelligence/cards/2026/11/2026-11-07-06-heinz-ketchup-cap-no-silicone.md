# Heinz spent 8 years removing one tiny part

**The find:** Heinz's flip-top ketchup cap used a tiny silicone valve for decades to control flow and stop drips. Heinz's own patent states that caps "lacking a silicon membrane" are "more easily recyclable," because silicone can't be mechanically separated from the plastic shell at recycling scale. Replacing the valve with pure internal channel geometry — exploiting ketchup's own thinning-under-pressure behavior — took eight years, 45 prototypes, 185,000 engineering hours and $1.2 million, eliminating roughly 300 million unrecyclable caps a year.
**The surprise:** The hardest, most expensive engineering problem wasn't making the cap work — it already worked — it was making it work with one fewer material.
**The idea:** [Logistics-driven design](../../../concepts/logistics-driven-design.md): the biggest hidden cost of a well-functioning design can be a tiny secondary material nobody notices, and removing it, not improving it, is sometimes the whole challenge.

## In depth

For decades, every Heinz squeeze bottle cap concealed a trick: a tiny silicone valve, molded into a polypropylene flip-top shell, that flexed open under squeeze pressure and sealed itself shut the instant you let go — the mechanism behind the "perfect squeeze" and the clean stop. That same silicone speck was also why the cap was trash. Silicone and polypropylene can't be mechanically separated at the scale and speed recycling facilities operate at, and the United States has, by most counts, effectively one plant that recycles silicone. So for years, roughly 300 million Heinz caps went to landfill annually even as the PET bottles around them got recycled. Heinz's own utility patent, US 12,686,535 B2 ("Container, closure, and methods for manufacture," filed January 2023, assigned to H.J. Heinz Company Brands LLC), states the problem plainly: the closure "may be formed with or without a silicon membrane or rubber valve," and "if the closure cap lacks a silicon membrane, the closure cap may be more easily recyclable."

Heinz and manufacturing partner Berry Global spent eight to nine years and, per Kraft Heinz's own figures, 45 prototypes, more than 185,000 engineering hours, and $1.2 million solving it — not by finding a recyclable substitute material, but by eliminating the second material altogether. The new cap is pure polypropylene, and it replaces the valve's job with pure geometry: an internal antechamber and a maze of channels that force ketchup along an indirect path before it reaches the nozzle. Ketchup is shear-thinning — it stays thick at rest but thins and flows under strain — so a firm squeeze pushes it through the constricted channels where it loosens and dispenses at a controlled rate, while at rest its regained viscosity plus the indirect path keeps it from dripping, no elastic membrane required. CT scans by the engineering firm Lumafield visually confirmed the shift: the old cap shows up as two distinct-density materials, the new one as a single uniform mass. Kraft Heinz senior packaging sustainability manager Trenten Huntington noted the team "tested over 45 prototypes to find the optimal solution that balances functionality, ease of use, and aesthetics." The cap launched in the UK on Heinz Tomato Ketchup bottles in mid-to-late 2023, expanded across more Heinz sauces in the UK and Europe through 2024-2025, and as of this writing has not been confirmed for a US rollout — meaning the fix for an American recycling gap debuted everywhere except America.
