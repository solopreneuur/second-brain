# Corning's glass with no customer

**The find:** Corning developed an ultra-strong chemically tempered glass, Chemcor, in 1960 under "Project Muscle," pitched it to automakers for windshields, got rejected, and shelved it for 36 years.
**The surprise:** In 2007 Steve Jobs asked Corning for scratch-resistant iPhone glass; Corning pulled the old Chemcor formula off the shelf and shipped it as Gorilla Glass, now on over 8 billion devices.
**The idea:** [Right idea, wrong time](../../../concepts/right-idea-wrong-time.md): a shelved invention isn't dead, it's waiting for a market that doesn't exist yet.

## In depth

In the early 1960s, Corning researchers developed an ion-exchange process that chemically strengthens glass: soaking it in a molten salt bath swaps smaller sodium ions near the surface for larger potassium ions, compressing the surface layer and making the glass far harder to scratch or crack. Corning marketed the result as Chemcor under an internal effort called Project Muscle, and pitched it to automakers as a windshield replacement. Ford and GM turned it down — the glass was tough, but when it did break, it failed in thin, dangerously sharp shards rather than the dull pebbles of laminated safety glass, and automakers weren't willing to pay a premium to solve a problem their existing glass already handled well enough. Chemcor found only scattered niche use (phone booth glass, some safety-glasses lenses) and sat shelved for roughly four decades.

In 2005–2007, Steve Jobs was finalizing the first iPhone and had watched a prototype scratch inside his own pocket; he decided a plastic display screen wasn't acceptable. With the ship date fixed for June 2007, Jobs called Corning CEO Wendell Weeks and demanded a thin, scratch-resistant glass that could be mass-produced within about six months. Weeks told him Corning didn't currently make anything like that — but remembered Chemcor sitting in the archives. Jobs reportedly told him "don't be afraid" and pushed him to commit anyway. Weeks assigned scientists Adam Ellison and Matt Dejneka to adapt the old 1960s chemistry to equipment Corning already owned, since there was no time to build new plants. Corning retrofit its Harrodsburg, Kentucky plant — built in 1952 — to run the new formula, and by June 2007 had produced enough of what was branded Gorilla Glass to cover roughly seven football fields. The first iPhone shipped on schedule with it.

Gorilla Glass has since become the default cover glass across the smartphone industry, used on more than 8 billion devices, now in its ninth major iteration.
