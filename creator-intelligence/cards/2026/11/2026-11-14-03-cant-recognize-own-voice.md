# You can't recognize your own voice

**The find:** Researchers played people short recordings mixed with other voices and asked them to pick out their own; only about 38% could identify it within 5 seconds.
**The surprise:** Recognizing your own voice sounds like the easiest task imaginable, and most people still fail it.
**The idea:** The voice you hear in your head (boosted by bone conduction) is a different signal than the one everyone else, including a recording, actually hears — "yourself" as others hear you is almost a stranger ([voice confrontation](../../../concepts/voice-confrontation.md)).

2 · Shocking number · Psychology · [Royal Society Open Science](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rsos/article/10/2/221561/91955/Bone-conduction-facilitates-self-other-voice)

## In depth

In 1967, psychologists Clyde Rousey and Philip Holzman published a study in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology that tested something almost nobody had measured before: can people actually recognize their own voice? They played subjects short clips — as brief as one second, up to five seconds — mixed in among recordings of other people's voices, then asked subjects to pick out which one was theirs. Only about 38% succeeded immediately. People didn't just hesitate; many confidently pointed to someone else's voice as their own.

The mechanism is physical, not psychological. When you speak, you hear yourself two ways at once: through air conduction (sound leaving your mouth, traveling through the air, entering your ear canal — what everyone else hears) and through bone conduction (vibrations traveling directly through your skull and jaw into your inner ear). Bone is a better conductor of low frequencies than air, so the skull adds a bass boost no outside listener ever gets. That's why your voice feels richer and deeper in your head than it sounds on a recording, which captures only the thinner, air-conducted signal everyone else hears. Rousey and Holzman called the resulting jolt of unfamiliarity and discomfort "voice confrontation" — in follow-up work (Holzman, Berger & Rousey, 1967) they found the discomfort was even sharper when people heard themselves speaking their native language versus a second language, tying the reaction to identity, not just acoustics.

Later neuroscience filled in why the brain treats self-generated speech differently at all: a mechanism called corollary discharge (or efference copy) means the motor cortex sends a predictive signal to the auditory cortex the instant before you speak, dampening the brain's response to sounds it expects to be self-made. fMRI work (Uddin et al., 2008) found the right inferior frontal gyrus activating specifically for self-voice recognition, and later work (Joos et al., 2021) showed sharper neural tuning for your own voice in the superior temporal gyrus. A 2023 U.S. survey of 1,522 adults found 57.5% disliked their recorded voice — evidence the mismatch Rousey and Holzman documented in 1967 is still nearly universal.
