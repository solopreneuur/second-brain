# The razor king wanted to abolish money

**The find:** A year before he invented the disposable razor, King Camp Gillette published "The Human Drift" (1894), a 131-page manifesto proposing to abolish money, private property and competition, replacing them with one giant public trust and a planned mega-city called "Metropolis," housing the entire US population at Niagara Falls.
**The surprise:** The man whose name became shorthand for a recurring-profit, pay-forever business model spent the year before that invention as a genuine anti-capitalist utopian author, and kept trying to fund "World Corporation" with his razor fortune for the rest of his life.

## In depth

In 1894, a year before his razor epiphany, traveling salesman King Camp Gillette published "The Human Drift" through Boston's New Era Publishing Co., a 131-page tract arguing that money, private property and competition should be scrapped in favor of a single public trust — which he called the "United Company" and later rebranded "World Corporation" — owning all production, with every citizen-shareholder getting one equal vote regardless of wealth. A large portion of the book described "Metropolis," a single mega-city at Niagara Falls meant to house the entire US population of roughly 60 million, built from glazed white porcelain tile on multi-level infrastructure and powered by the new Niagara hydroelectric system. The book found a real but modest audience: Edward Bellamy's Nationalist movement, built around his 1888 novel "Looking Backward," gave Gillette's ideas a sympathetic hearing, and a short-lived "Twentieth Century Company" formed partly in response, but investors never seriously backed it.

The following year, while shaving with a dulled blade in Brookline, Massachusetts, Gillette had his epiphany for a razor with cheap, disposable steel blades clipped into a reusable frame — a format he later credited to advice from his boss, William Painter, inventor of the disposable crown bottle cap at Crown Cork & Seal, who had urged him to invent something people would use once, throw away, and keep buying again. Gillette Safety Razor Company, founded in 1901, became the textbook case of exactly the kind of recurring-profit, competitive capitalism his own book had called to abolish — a contradiction historians note he never publicly resolved, instead treating the razor fortune as fuel for eventually funding World Corporation. He kept writing: "World Corporation" (1910) was a literal prospectus, and around that year he reportedly offered former president Theodore Roosevelt a million dollars to serve as the company's president over a planned colony in Arizona Territory; Roosevelt declined. Gillette's final book, "The People's Corporation" (1924), was co-written with Upton Sinclair and dedicated "To Mankind."
